Most young people avoid thinking about healthcare until they need it. By then, finding a GP who is accepting new patients, figuring out costs, and navigating the system feels unnecessarily complicated. Sorting this out now, when you are healthy, takes 30 minutes and saves significant stress later.

## Finding and registering with a GP

A GP (General Practitioner) is your primary care doctor — the first port of call for most health issues. Being enrolled with a GP reduces the cost of appointments significantly.

**How to find one:**

- Health Point (healthpoint.co.nz) lets you search for GPs in your area who are accepting new patients
- Ask people you know who they see and whether they would recommend them
- If you are a student, your tertiary institution may have a student health service which is often cheaper and convenient

**What to look for:**

- Accepting new patients (many practices have waitlists)
- Location — close to where you live or work
- Whether they have online booking and a patient portal (makes managing appointments much easier)
- Consultation fees — these vary significantly between practices

**Enrolling:** Contact the practice, ask if they are accepting new patients, and ask to enrol. You will need your NZ Health number (found on your Community Services Card if you have one, or they can look it up) and ID.

## What things cost

GP consultation costs vary. For enrolled patients, standard consultation fees are typically $20 to $50 in most areas, though this varies.

**Community Services Card (CSC):** If you are on a low income, a Community Services Card entitles you to lower-cost GP visits and prescriptions. Apply at Work and Income (workandincome.govt.nz) — it is free and worth doing if your income is below the threshold.

**Very Low Cost Access (VLCA) practices:** Some practices in lower-income areas have a Very Low Cost Access contract with the government, offering consultations for around $19.50 or less.

**Prescriptions:** The standard prescription co-payment is $5 per item (capped at a maximum of $100 per family per year). Some prescriptions are free.

## ACC — the Accident Compensation Corporation

ACC is one of New Zealand's most important and least understood institutions. It covers the cost of treatment for injuries caused by accidents — regardless of whether it was your fault, and regardless of your income or insurance status.

**What ACC covers:**

- Doctor visits, specialist consultations, and hospital treatment for injuries
- Physiotherapy and rehabilitation
- Loss of earnings if you cannot work due to an injury
- Surgery related to an accident
- Home help and modifications for serious injuries

**What it does not cover:**

- Illness (including mental illness, unless directly caused by an injury)
- Treatment for conditions that are not the result of an accident
- Cosmetic treatment

**How to claim:** When you see a doctor for an accident-related injury, tell them it was an accident. They will register an ACC claim. Most of the time you do not need to do anything — the practice handles it.

## When to go where

**Call 111:** Life-threatening emergencies. Chest pain, stroke symptoms (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty), serious injuries, difficulty breathing, unconsciousness.

**Emergency Department (ED):** Serious but not immediately life-threatening conditions — deep cuts, suspected fractures, high fever in infants, head injuries. Note: ED waits are often several hours for non-critical cases. It is also significantly more expensive than other options.

**Urgent care clinic:** Non-life-threatening conditions that cannot wait for a GP appointment — lacerations, minor fractures, infections, mild chest pain. Usually faster and cheaper than the ED for these situations.

**GP:** Ongoing health concerns, chronic conditions, referrals to specialists, mental health support, routine checks. Book in advance where possible.

**Telehealth:** Many GPs now offer phone or video consultations, often at a lower cost than in-person. Good for straightforward issues and follow-ups.

**Healthline:** 0800 611 116. Free 24/7 nurse advice line. If you are unsure whether something needs urgent attention, call here first.

## Getting the most out of a GP appointment

GP appointments are typically 15 minutes. To use them well:

- Know what you want to cover before you go — write it down if helpful
- If you have multiple concerns, mention them all at the start so the doctor can prioritise time
- Be specific: "I have had a sharp pain in my lower right abdomen for three days that is worse after eating" is more useful than "my stomach hurts"
- Ask questions if you do not understand something — it is your health
- If you are prescribed medication, ask what it does and what side effects to watch for
- Request a follow-up if your issue is not resolved

You are entitled to a second opinion if you are uncertain about a diagnosis or treatment plan.

## Mental health in the health system

If you are struggling with your mental health, start by talking to your GP. They can assess what you need, prescribe medication if appropriate, and refer you to mental health services.
