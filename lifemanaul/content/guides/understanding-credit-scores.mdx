Your credit score is a number you probably do not think about until you need a loan, a rental property, or occasionally even a phone plan — and then it matters a lot. Understanding how it works and how to build a good one from the start puts you in a significantly stronger position for major financial decisions later.

## What is a credit score?

A credit score is a number calculated by credit bureaus that summarises how reliably you repay debts. Lenders — banks, finance companies, landlords — use it to assess the risk of lending to you or entering a financial relationship with you.

In New Zealand, the main credit bureaus are Equifax (formerly Veda) and Centrix. Scores typically range from 0 to 1000, though the exact scale varies slightly by bureau.

Broadly:
- **Below 500:** Poor — will likely struggle to get credit approved
- **500 to 600:** Below average — may get credit but at higher interest rates
- **600 to 700:** Average — most standard credit will be accessible
- **700 to 800:** Good — strong approval chances, better rates
- **Above 800:** Excellent — best rates and terms available

## What goes into your score

Credit bureaus calculate your score based on your credit history. The main factors:

**Payment history (most important)** — whether you pay bills on time. Even one missed payment can significantly affect your score.

**Credit utilisation** — how much of your available credit you are using. Using less than 30% of your credit limit is considered good.

**Length of credit history** — older accounts generally help your score. This is why closing old accounts is not always a good idea.

**Types of credit** — a mix of credit types (credit card, personal loan, mortgage) can help, though this matters less for young people.

**New credit applications** — every time you apply for credit, a "hard inquiry" is recorded on your file. Multiple applications in a short period can lower your score.

**Defaults and court judgments** — unpaid debts that have been passed to a collection agency, or court judgments against you, significantly damage your score and remain on your file for up to 5 years.

## What appears on your credit report

Your credit report (different from your score — the report is the full record, the score is a summary number) shows:

- Your name, address history, date of birth
- All credit accounts, their limits, and payment history
- Defaults, collection activity, and court judgments
- Credit applications (hard inquiries)
- Any bankruptcy

## Checking your credit report

You are entitled to a free copy of your credit report from each bureau once every 12 months. You can also pay for more frequent access.

**How to get it free:**
- **Equifax NZ:** equifax.co.nz — free annual report
- **Centrix:** centrix.co.nz — free annual report

Check your report for errors — mistakes in credit reports are more common than you would expect, and an error that is not yours can unfairly damage your score. If you find an error, contact the bureau and the creditor to dispute it.

## Building good credit from scratch

Most young adults start with no credit history, which is different from bad credit — but it does mean you have not yet demonstrated reliability to lenders.

**Pay everything on time** — this is the most important thing you can do. Set up automatic payments for any regular bills so you cannot forget.

**Get a credit card and use it carefully** — a credit card used responsibly builds credit history. Spend a small amount each month (a regular purchase you would make anyway), and pay the balance in full every month. This demonstrates reliability without paying any interest.

**Do not apply for lots of credit at once** — each application leaves a hard inquiry on your file. Space applications out.

**Pay off any overdue debts** — if you have outstanding debts that have gone to collections, paying them off (even if they remain on your record for a period) stops further damage.

**Keep accounts open** — older accounts help your credit history length. Do not close old credit cards if there is no fee on them.

## Why it matters

Your credit score affects:

- Whether you can get a mortgage and at what interest rate
- Whether you can rent a property (many landlords check credit)
- Whether you can get a phone plan on credit
- Whether you can get personal loans or car finance
- Sometimes, whether you can get certain jobs (particularly in finance)

Building good credit is not complicated — it is mostly about paying things on time. The best time to start building that record is now, before you need to rely on it for something important.
