Voting takes about five minutes. Understanding how it works takes about ten. This guide covers both.

## Why younger people not voting matters

Before the how, the why. NZ general elections are held every three years. Consistently, voters aged 18 to 34 participate at significantly lower rates than older voters. In the 2023 election, turnout among 18 to 24 year olds was around 65% compared to over 85% for those over 65.

Politicians know this. It means they have a financial incentive to design policies that appeal to older, reliable voters — housing, superannuation, healthcare spending — and less incentive to prioritise things that disproportionately affect younger people, like housing affordability, student debt, and climate change.

When young people do not vote, they are not being neutral. They are handing their share of influence to other people.

## Enrolling to vote

You must be enrolled to vote in NZ elections. Enrolment is compulsory by law, though there is no penalty for not enrolling. Voting itself is not compulsory.

**Who can enrol:**
- NZ citizens or permanent residents
- Aged 18 or over (you can enrol at 17 but cannot vote until you turn 18)
- Living in NZ or overseas NZ citizens/residents who have been in NZ in the last 3 years

**How to enrol:**
Go to vote.nz and complete the online form. You will need your full name, date of birth, address, and NZ citizenship or residency details.

You can also enrol at a PostShop or at a polling place on election day (special vote).

Update your enrolment whenever you move address — the electorate you are enrolled in determines which ballot paper you receive.

## How MMP works

NZ uses Mixed Member Proportional (MMP) representation. You get two votes:

**Electorate vote** — you vote for a specific candidate to represent your local electorate in Parliament. The candidate who gets the most votes in your electorate wins the seat.

**Party vote** — you vote for a political party. This determines the overall composition of Parliament. A party that receives 5% of the party vote (or wins an electorate seat) gets seats in Parliament proportional to its party vote share.

**Example:** If a party gets 30% of the party vote, it gets roughly 30% of the 120 seats in Parliament (36 seats), regardless of how many electorate seats it wins.

**Which vote matters more?** For most parties, the party vote is more important because it directly determines how many MPs they have in total. Your electorate vote matters more in competitive electorates where the margin is narrow.

## Reading the ballot paper

You will receive two separate ballot papers — one green (electorate) and one orange (party).

**Electorate ballot:** A list of candidates from your electorate. Vote for one by putting a tick or cross in the circle next to their name.

**Party ballot:** A list of registered parties. Vote for one party by putting a tick or cross in the circle next to the party name.

You can split your vote — voting for a candidate from one party in your electorate and a different party on the party vote. This is common and legal.

## On election day

Polling places are open from 9am to 7pm on election day. You can find your polling place at vote.nz.

Bring your voting document if you received one in the mail. If you do not have one, you can still vote — just tell the electoral officer your name and address.

If you cannot make it on election day, advance voting is available for several weeks beforehand at various locations.

## Party policies

Before voting, it is worth spending 30 minutes understanding where the main parties stand on issues you care about. Good places to start:

- **vote.nz** — official Electoral Commission resource with party information
- **Newsroom, RNZ, Stuff** — election coverage from multiple perspectives
- **Policy.nz** — compares party policies by topic

You do not need to be a political expert to vote. You just need to know enough about what matters to you.

## Local elections

NZ also has local government elections every three years (alternating with general elections). These cover your local council, regional council, and District Health Board (where applicable). Turnout in local elections is even lower than in general elections — often under 40%.

Local elections decide things that affect you very directly: local planning, public transport, rates, parks, and local services. They are worth participating in.

Local election voting is done by postal ballot — you receive your voting papers in the mail and return them by a specified date.
