Knowing basic first aid is one of the most useful things you can learn. In an emergency, the first few minutes matter enormously — and the person closest to the emergency is usually not a doctor. That person might be you.

This guide covers the situations you are most likely to encounter. It is not a substitute for a first aid course (St John runs free or low-cost courses throughout NZ and they are worth doing), but it gives you the basics to act when it matters.

## The most important thing: call 111

In New Zealand, 111 is the emergency number for police, fire, and ambulance. In any serious emergency, call it immediately. Do not assume someone else has called. Do not spend time trying to figure out how serious it is before calling — call first, assess second.

When you call, the dispatcher will talk you through what to do. They are trained for this. Stay on the line.

## CPR (Cardiopulmonary resuscitation)

CPR is used when someone has stopped breathing and their heart has stopped. It keeps blood circulating until a defibrillator or paramedics can restart the heart.

**How to do CPR on an adult:**

1. Check the scene is safe, then check if the person is responsive — tap their shoulder and shout "Are you okay?"
2. If no response, call 111 (or ask someone else to while you start CPR)
3. Lay the person on their back on a firm, flat surface
4. Place the heel of one hand on the centre of their chest (lower half of the breastbone), place your other hand on top, and interlock your fingers
5. With straight arms, press down firmly and quickly — about 5 to 6 centimetres deep — and release. This is one compression
6. Do 30 compressions at a rate of about 100 to 120 per minute (roughly to the beat of Stayin' Alive by the Bee Gees)
7. After 30 compressions, tilt the head back gently, lift the chin, pinch the nose, and give 2 rescue breaths — enough to see the chest rise
8. Continue cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths until the ambulance arrives or the person recovers

If you are not comfortable doing rescue breaths, compression-only CPR (continuous chest compressions without the breaths) is still significantly better than nothing.

## Serious bleeding

For a wound that is bleeding heavily:

1. Apply firm, continuous pressure directly to the wound using whatever you have — a cloth, clothing, your hand
2. Do not remove the material if it soaks through — add more on top
3. Keep applying pressure without stopping for at least 10 minutes
4. If possible, raise the injured limb above the level of the heart
5. Call 111 if the bleeding is severe or does not slow down

Do not apply a tourniquet unless you have been trained to do so and bleeding cannot be controlled any other way.

## Burns

1. Remove the person from danger and remove any clothing or jewellery near the burn (unless it is stuck to the skin)
2. Cool the burn with cool (not cold) running water for 20 minutes — this is the most important step
3. Do not use ice, butter, toothpaste, or any home remedies — they make burns worse
4. Cover the burn loosely with cling wrap or a clean non-fluffy cloth
5. Call 111 or go to an emergency department for any burn larger than a 50-cent coin, burns on the face, hands, feet, or genitals, or burns that are white or brown rather than red

## Choking

**If the person can cough, let them cough** — a cough is more effective than anything you can do. Encourage them and watch closely.

**If the person cannot cough, speak, or breathe:**

1. Call 111 immediately (or ask someone else to)
2. Give 5 back blows — lean them forward and strike firmly between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand
3. Give 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich manoeuvre) — stand behind them, make a fist just above the navel, grab it with your other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward
4. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is dislodged or they lose consciousness
5. If they lose consciousness, begin CPR and check the mouth before giving rescue breaths

**For infants under 1 year:** Do not use abdominal thrusts. Use 5 back blows followed by 5 chest thrusts.

## Fainting

1. If someone feels faint, help them sit or lie down before they fall
2. If they have fainted, check they are breathing
3. Raise their legs above the level of their heart if possible
4. Loosen tight clothing
5. Do not give them food or water immediately
6. Call 111 if they do not regain consciousness within a minute, or if they faint repeatedly

## Allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction. Signs include swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, hives, and collapse.

If someone has an EpiPen (adrenaline auto-injector), use it — jab it into the outer thigh, even through clothing. Call 111 immediately after using it. Lay the person flat with legs raised (unless they are having difficulty breathing, in which case sit them up).

## The most important thing to remember

Doing something imperfectly is almost always better than doing nothing. If you are not sure whether CPR compressions are deep enough, or whether you have the technique exactly right, do it anyway. People have survived because a bystander tried.

Consider doing a St John First Aid course — they offer Youth First Aid programmes throughout New Zealand and they are genuinely worth your time.
