Many New Zealanders live together without getting married, assuming their legal situation is simply "not married." In reality, after a qualifying period, de facto relationships in NZ carry very similar legal rights and obligations to marriage — particularly when it comes to property.

Understanding this before you are in the middle of a separation is significantly better than understanding it after.

## What is a de facto relationship?

Under the Property (Relationships) Act 1976, a de facto relationship is one where two people:

- Are not married to each other or in a civil union
- Are living together as a couple in a genuine domestic relationship

Factors the courts consider when determining whether a de facto relationship exists include: the duration of the relationship, whether there is a shared residence, the financial arrangements between the parties, sexual relationship, care of children, and public perception of the relationship.

## The 3-year threshold

The Property (Relationships) Act applies to de facto couples who have been in a qualifying relationship for at least 3 years. Below 3 years, different (more limited) rules apply.

The 3-year period can effectively be reached earlier if:
- You have a child together
- One person has made a significant contribution to the relationship (financially or otherwise) and a failure to divide property equally would result in serious injustice

## What is relationship property?

When a qualifying de facto relationship ends (by separation or death), the default rule is that relationship property is divided equally — 50/50.

**Relationship property broadly includes:**

- The family home, regardless of whose name it is in
- The family chattels (furniture, household items, vehicles used by the family)
- Property acquired by either partner during the relationship
- KiwiSaver contributions made during the relationship
- Income earned during the relationship and assets purchased with it
- Business interests acquired during the relationship

**Separate property (not divided equally) broadly includes:**

- Property owned before the relationship
- Property received as a gift or inheritance (if kept separate)
- Property acquired after separation

The distinction between relationship and separate property can be complicated in practice — particularly if separate property has been mixed with relationship property (for example, using pre-relationship savings to renovate the shared home).

## The family home

The family home receives special treatment. Even if only one partner's name is on the title, the family home is generally treated as relationship property and divided 50/50. This applies regardless of who earned the money to buy it or who has made more mortgage payments.

This surprises many people. If you buy a house before your relationship reaches the 3-year threshold, and the relationship later ends, the family home is still treated as relationship property. The start date of the relationship matters, not the purchase date.

## What happens when a de facto relationship ends

If the relationship ends by separation, either partner can apply to court for a division of relationship property under the Act. The default is 50/50 unless there are exceptional circumstances.

If one partner dies, the surviving partner has rights under the Act — including the right to apply for 50% of relationship property. This is separate from any will.

## Contracting out agreements (prenups)

A contracting out agreement (colloquially called a prenuptial or de facto agreement) is a legal document where both parties agree to different rules for how property would be divided if the relationship ends.

Common uses include:
- Protecting property brought into the relationship (particularly a family home or significant assets)
- Protecting an inheritance received during the relationship
- Providing for an unequal split where one partner has significantly more assets

For a contracting out agreement to be enforceable in NZ:
- Both parties must have received independent legal advice
- Both parties must have signed the agreement and had their signatures witnessed by a lawyer
- The agreement must be in writing

Without those formalities, the agreement is not enforceable.

## Practical implications

**If you own a home and enter a de facto relationship:** Your home may become relationship property after 3 years of cohabitation. If you want to protect your pre-relationship equity, a contracting out agreement is the mechanism.

**If you are buying a home together:** Consider whose name goes on the title, what happens to individual contributions (e.g., one person contributed a larger deposit), and whether you need a co-ownership agreement or lawyer's advice.

**KiwiSaver:** Contributions made during the relationship are relationship property. This includes the returns on those contributions. If you separate, the relationship-period portion of one partner's KiwiSaver can be transferred to the other.

**If your relationship is approaching 3 years:** If there is significant property involved and you have not had a conversation about expectations on separation, this is a good time to do so.

## Getting advice

The Property (Relationships) Act has significant financial consequences and the details matter. For anything beyond general understanding:

- **A family lawyer** — the right professional for de facto property advice and for drafting contracting out agreements
- **Citizens Advice Bureau** — free general information
- **Community Law Centres** — free legal advice for people who meet income criteria

This is an area where the cost of professional advice is almost always worth it relative to what is at stake.
