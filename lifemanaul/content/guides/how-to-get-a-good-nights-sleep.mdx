If you struggle to fall asleep, find yourself exhausted in the morning, or feel like you could sleep until noon on weekends, you are not lazy. There is a biological reason for all of it — and there are practical things that actually help.

## How much sleep do teenagers need?

Adults need about 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night. Teenagers need more — between 8 and 10 hours. This is not a preference. During puberty, your brain is developing rapidly and sleep is when most of that development happens. Skimping on sleep during your teenage years has real consequences for memory, concentration, mood, and physical health.

Most teenagers are significantly sleep deprived. Studies consistently show that fewer than 1 in 5 teenagers get the recommended amount of sleep on school nights.

## Why teenagers naturally stay up late

Here is something important that most adults do not explain: it is not just laziness or bad habits that make teenagers want to stay up late. During puberty, a biological shift happens in your circadian rhythm — the internal clock that tells your body when to sleep.

Before puberty, children naturally want to sleep and wake early. During puberty, the release of melatonin (the hormone that makes you feel sleepy) shifts to about two hours later. This means your body genuinely does not feel tired until later at night — and you naturally want to wake up later in the morning.

This is called sleep phase delay and it is real, documented biology. It is why early school start times are controversial — they are literally working against how teenage bodies are programmed.

## Why sleep matters so much

When you are sleep deprived, you are not just tired. You are:

- Less able to concentrate and learn
- More emotionally reactive and irritable
- More likely to make poor decisions
- More prone to anxiety and low mood
- At higher risk of accidents
- More likely to get sick

One bad night is manageable. Weeks or months of inadequate sleep compound into serious effects on your mental and physical health.

## What gets in the way of sleep

**Screens** — phones, tablets, and computers emit blue light that suppresses melatonin and signals to your brain that it is daytime. Scrolling is also mentally stimulating and activates the reward system in your brain, making it harder to wind down. This is the biggest sleep disruptor for most teenagers.

**Caffeine** — coffee, energy drinks, and many soft drinks contain caffeine, which has a half-life of about 5 to 6 hours. A coffee at 4pm still has half its caffeine in your system at 10pm.

**Irregular sleep times** — sleeping in on weekends confuses your body clock. The Sunday-night insomnia many people experience is largely caused by sleeping in on Saturday and Sunday mornings.

**Stress and anxiety** — if your mind is racing with worries when you lie down, sleep becomes much harder. See our guide on managing anxiety for strategies.

**Napping too long or too late** — a short nap (20 minutes) in the early afternoon can help. A two-hour nap at 5pm will wreck your night.

## What actually helps

**Keep consistent sleep and wake times** — this is the most evidence-backed sleep intervention available. Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day (including weekends, or as close to it as you can manage) regulates your body clock more effectively than anything else.

**Put your phone in another room** — this is hard but effective. The bed should be associated with sleep. If your phone is within reach, you will use it.

**Wind down for 30 minutes before bed** — dim the lights, do something calm. Reading a physical book, light stretching, or listening to quiet music all work. Avoid anything that gets your heart rate or mind racing.

**Keep your room cool and dark** — your body temperature drops slightly as you fall asleep. A cool room (around 18 degrees) helps this happen. Darkness signals to your brain that it is time to produce melatonin.

**Get outside in the morning** — morning light is the most powerful signal your body clock has. Even 10 minutes of natural light in the morning helps anchor your circadian rhythm.

**Avoid caffeine after 2pm** — this gives it time to clear your system before bed.

## What about melatonin supplements?

Melatonin supplements are available in NZ and can help in specific situations — particularly jet lag or adjusting your sleep schedule. They are not a long-term fix for chronic sleep problems. If you are considering them, talk to a doctor or pharmacist first.

## When sleep problems are more serious

If you have consistently tried good sleep habits for several weeks and still cannot sleep, or if you experience symptoms like waking repeatedly in the night, very early morning waking, or sleeping excessively (more than 10 hours regularly), talk to a doctor. Sleep disorders are real and treatable.

Persistent sleep problems can also be a sign of anxiety or depression, which are also very treatable. The key is not to just push through and assume things will improve on their own.
