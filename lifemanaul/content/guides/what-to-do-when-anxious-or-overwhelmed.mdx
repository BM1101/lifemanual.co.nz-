Feeling anxious or overwhelmed sometimes is part of being human. But if it is happening a lot, or stopping you from doing things you want to do, there are practical strategies that genuinely help — and knowing when to reach out for support.

## What is anxiety?

Anxiety is your body's alarm system. When your brain perceives a threat — whether it is a tiger, a test, or a social situation — it triggers a physical response: heart rate speeds up, muscles tense, breathing quickens. This is the fight-or-flight response and it evolved to help us survive danger.

The problem is that the brain does not always distinguish between real physical danger and social or emotional threat. Your body can react to a presentation at school the same way it would react to genuine danger.

Anxiety becomes a problem when:
- It is disproportionate to the actual situation
- It happens frequently without a clear cause
- It stops you from doing things you want or need to do
- It is affecting your sleep, concentration, or relationships

## In-the-moment techniques

When you feel anxiety rising, these techniques can interrupt the physical response:

**Controlled breathing** — slow, deliberate breathing directly activates the parasympathetic nervous system, which counters the fight-or-flight response. Try breathing in for 4 counts, holding for 4, and breathing out for 6. The longer exhale is key. Do this for 2 to 3 minutes.

**The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique** — notice 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, and 1 you can taste. This pulls your attention into the present moment and interrupts the spiral of anxious thoughts.

**Cold water** — splashing cold water on your face or holding something cold activates the dive reflex, which slows the heart rate. It sounds simple because it is, and it works.

**Moving your body** — even a short walk outside can significantly reduce acute anxiety by burning off the adrenaline your body has produced.

## Longer-term strategies

In-the-moment techniques manage the symptoms. These strategies address the patterns:

**Face the things that make you anxious, gradually** — avoidance feels like relief in the short term, but it teaches your brain that the thing is genuinely dangerous. Every time you avoid something, the anxiety around it gets stronger. Gradually exposing yourself to uncomfortable situations (starting with less scary versions) is the most effective way to reduce anxiety over time.

**Challenge anxious thoughts** — when you notice an anxious thought, ask: Is this definitely true? What is the evidence for and against it? What would I say to a friend thinking this? Anxious thoughts are often catastrophic predictions that do not reflect reality.

**Reduce caffeine** — caffeine increases heart rate and can trigger or worsen anxiety, particularly in people who are already prone to it.

**Protect your sleep** — sleep deprivation and anxiety are closely linked. When you are tired, your brain is less able to regulate emotions. See our sleep guide for practical help.

**Limit social media** — research consistently links heavy social media use with increased anxiety and lower self-esteem in teenagers. Consider setting daily time limits.

## When to ask for help

It is time to talk to someone if:
- Anxiety is happening most days
- It is stopping you from going to school, seeing friends, or doing normal activities
- You are using alcohol or other substances to manage how you feel
- You are having thoughts of hurting yourself

In New Zealand, the following are free and confidential:
- **Text 1737** any time to talk to a trained counsellor
- **Youthline**: 0800 376 633 or text 234
- **Your school counsellor** — they are there specifically for this

Asking for help is not weakness. It is self-awareness — recognising that you could use support and doing something about it. Most anxiety is very treatable, and the earlier you address it, the better.

## A note on anxiety vs worry

Worry is normal. Having a test tomorrow and feeling nervous is appropriate and proportionate. Anxiety is when the nervous feeling does not match the actual threat, persists after the situation has passed, or happens without a clear trigger.

If you are not sure whether what you are experiencing is normal worry or something that needs support, that uncertainty alone is a reason to talk to someone. They can help you figure it out.
