Puberty is the process your body goes through as it changes from a child's body into an adult's. It is driven by hormones — chemical messengers that your brain starts releasing at a certain point, triggering a series of changes that happen over several years.

It can feel overwhelming, confusing, or embarrassing. That is completely normal. Almost every adult alive has been through exactly what you are going through, and most of them found it uncomfortable too. This guide explains what is happening and why, so it feels less mysterious.

## When does puberty start?

There is no single answer. Puberty can begin anywhere between ages 8 and 16, and both early and late starts are completely normal. Girls typically begin puberty earlier than boys — usually between 8 and 13. Boys usually start between 9 and 14.

If you are 15 and nothing seems to be happening, or if you started at 8 and feel ahead of everyone else, talk to a doctor. But in most cases, the timing is simply your body doing things at its own pace.

## What happens in girls

The first sign of puberty in girls is usually breast development — often starting as a tender, small lump under one or both nipples. This is called a breast bud and it is normal for one side to develop before the other.

Other changes include:
- Growth of pubic and underarm hair
- A growth spurt — often the fastest height gain of your life
- Widening of the hips
- The beginning of your menstrual cycle (periods)
- Increased sweating and changes to body odour
- Skin changes, including acne

Periods usually begin about two years after breast development starts. See our guide on female cycles for a full explanation of how periods work.

## What happens in boys

The first sign of puberty in boys is usually growth of the testicles. This is followed by:
- Growth of pubic, underarm, and facial hair
- Growth of the penis
- A growth spurt — boys often grow significantly taller over a short period
- Voice changes — the voice "breaks" and deepens, sometimes cracking mid-sentence
- Increased sweating and body odour
- Skin changes, including acne
- Erections and ejaculation, including wet dreams (ejaculation during sleep)

Wet dreams are completely normal and not something you can control. They happen because your body is producing sperm for the first time.

## What happens to everyone

Some changes happen regardless of sex:
- **Acne** — spots and pimples are caused by increased oil production in the skin. They are very common and usually manageable with a good skincare routine.
- **Body odour** — your sweat glands become more active. Using deodorant or antiperspirant daily helps.
- **Mood changes** — you may feel more irritable, emotional, self-conscious, or sensitive than before. This is real and it is caused by hormones — not a character flaw.
- **Interest in relationships** — it is normal to start thinking about romantic or sexual feelings during puberty.

## The emotional side

Puberty is not just physical. The hormones driving physical changes also affect your brain. You may find that:

- Emotions feel more intense — things that did not used to bother you now feel huge
- You care more about what other people think of you
- You want more independence from your parents
- You feel more self-conscious about your body
- Your friendships feel more complicated

All of this is a normal part of development. The part of your brain responsible for regulating emotions and making decisions is still developing throughout your teens — which is why things can feel harder to manage right now.

## What is not normal

While puberty involves a wide range of experiences, some things are worth mentioning to a doctor:

- Puberty starting before age 8 in girls or age 9 in boys
- No signs of puberty by age 16
- Significant pain or unusual lumps
- Very heavy or very irregular periods (after the first year or two)
- Extreme mood changes that interfere with daily life

Doctors have seen all of this before. There is nothing embarrassing about asking.

## Talking to someone

If you are confused or concerned about anything happening to your body, talk to a trusted adult — a parent, school counsellor, or doctor. If that feels uncomfortable, you can also call or text a helpline anonymously.

In New Zealand, you can text 1737 any time to talk to a trained counsellor for free.

You are not the first person to find this confusing. You will not be the last.
