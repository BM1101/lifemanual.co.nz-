Turning 65 is the point at which your KiwiSaver becomes accessible, but "accessible" does not mean "you must withdraw it" — and the decision about how and when to draw it down is one of the more consequential financial choices of this life stage.

## What actually happens at 65

Once you reach 65 and have been a KiwiSaver member for at least 5 years (the minimum membership period, which can extend past 65 if you joined later in life), you can withdraw some or all of your balance at any time, in whatever amounts you choose. There is no requirement to withdraw everything at once, and no requirement to stop contributing either — you can continue working and contributing to KiwiSaver past 65 if you want to, including continuing to receive employer contributions where applicable (though the government contribution stops at 65).

## Why a full lump-sum withdrawal is rarely the best default

A common instinct is to withdraw the full balance at 65, treating it like a lump sum that has finally "matured." For most people this is not the optimal approach, for a few reasons:

- It removes the balance from what may still be appropriate long-term investment growth, particularly since many people now live 20 to 30 years past 65
- A large lump sum sitting in a bank account earns comparatively little and may be exposed to inflation eroding its value over time
- It removes the flexibility of staged withdrawals, which can be structured to manage tax and cashflow more efficiently than a single lump sum

## Staged withdrawal approaches

Many KiwiSaver providers offer structured withdrawal options once you reach 65 — regular automatic payments (weekly, fortnightly, or monthly) similar to a wage, which can be adjusted over time. This approach:

- Keeps the remaining balance invested and potentially still growing
- Provides predictable, budgetable income alongside NZ Super
- Can be adjusted if your spending needs change, rather than being locked into decisions made at a single point in time

## Adjusting your fund type as you approach and enter retirement

The general principle of shifting from growth-oriented funds toward more conservative settings as you approach the point where you need the money still applies at and after 65 — but "conservative" does not necessarily mean "all cash." With a retirement that may span two or three decades, having some ongoing growth exposure can help your balance keep pace with inflation and last longer, provided the overall risk level matches your comfort and need for stability. Many providers offer specific "retirement" or "transition" fund options designed for this stage — worth reviewing rather than assuming your existing fund choice from your working years is still the right fit.

## Combining KiwiSaver with NZ Super

Because NZ Super is paid at a flat rate regardless of other income or assets, your KiwiSaver drawdown effectively becomes the flexible, adjustable part of your retirement income, while NZ Super provides a stable base. Some people choose to draw down KiwiSaver more heavily in the earlier, often more active years of retirement, and rely more on NZ Super alone in later years — this is a reasonable strategy, but should be modelled with actual numbers rather than assumed, since running down KiwiSaver too quickly is a genuine risk if you live longer than planned for.

## Getting a plan rather than deciding on the day

Because turning 65 does not force an immediate decision, many people delay thinking about their withdrawal strategy until they need to make one on short notice. A better approach is to model your likely retirement income needs, your NZ Super entitlement, and your KiwiSaver balance together — ideally with a financial adviser — a year or two before you actually reach 65, so the withdrawal approach is a deliberate plan rather than a reaction.
