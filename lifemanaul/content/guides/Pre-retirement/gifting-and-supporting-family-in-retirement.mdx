Requests for financial help from adult children — a house deposit, help through a rough patch, support with grandchildren's costs — often land in exactly the years when you are also trying to firm up your own retirement plan. Both things matter, but working through the decision deliberately produces better outcomes than deciding under emotional pressure in the moment.

## Start with your own numbers first

Before deciding how much, if anything, you can offer, it is worth having a reasonably clear picture of your own retirement income plan (see our related article on building a drawdown plan) so that any gift or loan decision is made against real numbers rather than a general sense of "we should be able to help." A specific number — what you can genuinely afford to give without compromising your own plan — is a far more useful starting point for the conversation than an open-ended willingness to help.

## Gift or loan — and why the distinction matters

A straightforward gift is simple in the moment but has no path back if your own circumstances change later or if the relationship dynamics shift. A structured, documented loan — even a modest, informal one between family members — gives both parties clarity and, if needed, a legitimate way to have some of that money returned. This does not need to be adversarial or overly formal; even a simple written agreement noting the amount, whether interest applies, and expected repayment terms (even informally, such as "when the property sells" or "no fixed date") provides useful clarity that a purely verbal understanding does not.

## The deprivation of assets consideration

If you may need residential care in the future, be aware that Work and Income's means assessment includes provisions around "deprivation of assets" — meaning gifts made, particularly larger ones made close to when care becomes necessary, can be treated as if you still held those assets when assessing your eligibility for a care subsidy. There are permitted annual gifting thresholds that are generally accepted without scrutiny, but larger gifts made without regard to this can have real, unintended consequences for your own future care funding. This is worth understanding and, where a significant gift is being considered, discussing with an adviser familiar with these rules before proceeding.

## Common forms of family support and their trade-offs

**Deposit assistance for a first home** — one of the most common requests. Options range from an outright gift, to a documented family loan, to acting as a guarantor on a portion of a mortgage (which carries its own risks, since you become liable if repayments are missed).

**Ongoing support during a difficult period** — job loss, relationship breakdown, or unexpected costs. Worth treating as a bounded, time-limited arrangement with an honest conversation about what is sustainable, rather than an open-ended commitment that quietly becomes permanent.

**Grandchildren's costs** — childcare, school costs, or activities. Easy to say yes to in isolation, but worth considering cumulatively rather than each request in isolation, since the total can add up to a meaningful ongoing drain on retirement income without ever feeling like a single large decision.

## It is legitimate to say no, or to offer less than asked

Many parents feel real pressure to help regardless of their own position, and struggle to say no or to offer a smaller amount than requested. Protecting your own retirement security is not a selfish choice — being financially dependent on your own adult children later in life, because your resources were exhausted helping them earlier, is a worse outcome for everyone than a boundary set clearly and kindly now. Being direct about your own numbers and limits, rather than vague or guilt-driven, tends to be received better by adult children than people expect, and avoids the resentment that can build from either overextending yourself or extending help inconsistently based on emotion rather than a clear position.

## A reasonable approach

Decide your own affordability in advance of any specific request, be open with family about the general shape of your position (without necessarily disclosing every detail), prefer documented arrangements over purely informal ones for anything beyond a modest amount, and treat each request as an opportunity to apply a considered position rather than a one-off decision made under emotional pressure each time.
