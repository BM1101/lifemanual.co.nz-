It is easy to plan for aging parents' care needs while assuming your own future care will simply sort itself out later. Turning the same planning lens on your own situation, while you still have full capacity and choice, produces a meaningfully better outcome than leaving these decisions for a crisis to force.

## How residential care funding actually works

If residential care becomes necessary, costs are assessed through a Work and Income financial means assessment, which looks at both income and assets against set thresholds. If your assets are above the relevant threshold, you are expected to contribute toward your care costs from your own means before a government subsidy applies; below the threshold, more government support is available. These thresholds and rules change periodically, so getting a current assessment or advice close to when it becomes relevant — rather than relying on general figures from years earlier — matters.

## The family home rules

One of the most important and commonly misunderstood parts of this system concerns the family home. Broadly, the home may be exempt from the assets test if a spouse, partner, or certain dependent relatives continue to live in it after you move into care — but the specific conditions matter considerably, and asset planning done purely to try to qualify for a subsidy (such as gifting the home to children shortly before needing care) is scrutinised and can be treated as a "deprivation of assets," which can affect eligibility rather than help it. This is an area where getting a proper assessment or specialist elder law advice well in advance, rather than making decisions reactively, is genuinely important.

## Setting up your own Enduring Power of Attorney

An Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA) needs to be set up while you still have legal capacity — it cannot be arranged after that capacity is lost. Setting up both a property/finances EPA and a personal care and welfare EPA now, naming who you would want to make decisions on your behalf, is one of the highest-value and lowest-cost pieces of planning available at this stage. Without it, your family would need to apply to the Family Court for authority to act for you, which is slower, more expensive, and considerably more stressful during what is already a difficult time.

## Having the conversation with your adult children

Many people avoid discussing their own future care wishes with their children, either because it feels premature or because it feels uncomfortable to raise. In practice, having this conversation while you are well — covering your preferences around staying home versus residential care, your financial situation in broad terms, where key documents are kept, and who you would want making decisions if needed — removes an enormous amount of guesswork and potential family conflict later. Framing it as similar to organising a will (routine, sensible planning rather than a sign of decline) tends to make the conversation easier to start.

## Documents and information worth organising now

- Your will, kept current (see our related article on updating wills in mid-life)
- Your Enduring Power of Attorney documents, for both property and welfare
- A clear record of your financial position, or at least where to find it — bank accounts, KiwiSaver provider, any investments
- Your preferences around care, written down or at least clearly discussed, even informally

## The general principle

Planning for your own aged care is not about assuming the worst or dwelling on decline — it is closer to organising your other important paperwork: something that matters most exactly when you are least able to attend to it yourself, which is precisely why doing it now, while everything is straightforward, is worth prioritising over waiting for a clearer signal that it is needed.
