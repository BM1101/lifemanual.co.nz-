If you have recently got your period, or are expecting it soon, this guide explains everything in plain English — how it works, what to expect, and when to ask for help.

## What is a period?

Every month, your uterus prepares for a possible pregnancy by building up a lining of blood and tissue. If no pregnancy occurs, that lining is shed through the vagina. This is your period.

A period is not an injury or an illness. It is a sign that your reproductive system is working as it should.

## How long is a cycle?

A menstrual cycle is measured from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. The average cycle is 28 days, but anywhere between 21 and 35 days is normal.

When periods first start, they are often irregular — sometimes skipping months entirely. This is normal and usually settles down within one to two years.

## How long does a period last?

Most periods last between three and seven days. The flow is usually heavier in the first couple of days and lighter toward the end.

## What does the blood look like?

Period blood can range from bright red to dark brown or even almost black, especially at the start or end. This is all normal. Dark blood is simply older blood that has taken longer to leave the body.

Small clots (up to about the size of a 50-cent coin) are also normal, especially on heavier days.

## What does a period feel like?

Many people experience some discomfort before or during their period. Common symptoms include:

- Cramps in the lower abdomen or back
- Bloating
- Breast tenderness
- Headaches
- Mood changes, irritability, or feeling emotional
- Tiredness

These symptoms are caused by hormones and are referred to as premenstrual syndrome (PMS) when they occur in the days before a period.

## Managing period pain

Mild to moderate cramps are common and can usually be managed with:

- Over-the-counter pain relief such as ibuprofen (take it at the first sign of cramping, before the pain peaks)
- A heat pack or hot water bottle on the lower abdomen
- Gentle movement or walking
- Rest

If pain is severe enough to stop you going to school or doing normal activities, or if it does not respond to pain relief, talk to a doctor. Painful periods can sometimes be a sign of a condition called endometriosis, which is treatable.

## Period products

There are several options for managing your period:

**Pads** — worn in underwear, absorbing blood outside the body. Available in different sizes for different flow levels. A good starting point.

**Tampons** — inserted into the vagina to absorb blood internally. They do not hurt if inserted correctly and do not affect virginity. Change every 4 to 8 hours and never leave one in for more than 8 hours.

**Period underwear** — absorbent underwear that replaces or supplements other products. Washable and reusable.

**Menstrual cups** — small silicone cups inserted into the vagina. Can be worn for up to 12 hours and reused for years.

Try different options and find what works for you. Many people use a combination.

## Tracking your cycle

Tracking your period helps you understand your body, predict when it is coming, and notice anything unusual. You can use a simple app (Clue and Flo are both good) or just note the dates in your phone calendar.

Track: the first day of your period, how many days it lasts, and how heavy it is. Over a few months you will start to see your pattern.

## When to see a doctor

Talk to a doctor if:
- Your period has not started by age 16
- Periods are extremely heavy (soaking through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours)
- You have severe pain that does not respond to pain relief
- Your cycle is consistently shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days
- Your period suddenly stops for more than three months (and you are not pregnant)
- You have unusual discharge or itching

All of these are treatable. Doctors talk about periods constantly — there is nothing embarrassing about bringing it up.

## Talking about it

Periods are a normal part of life for roughly half the world's population. If you feel uncomfortable discussing them, you are not alone — but the more you can talk about your body with trusted people (a parent, school nurse, or doctor), the better equipped you will be to look after yourself.

In New Zealand, you can also call Healthline on 0800 611 116 any time for free health advice.
