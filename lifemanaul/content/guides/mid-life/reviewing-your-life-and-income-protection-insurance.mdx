Insurance is one of those things people set up once — often when taking out a first mortgage or having a first child — and then rarely revisit. By your 40s or 50s, it is worth a proper look, because both your needs and the products available have likely changed considerably since you last paid attention.

## Why old cover levels stop making sense

If your life insurance was sized to cover a $400,000 mortgage and young children a decade or more ago, and your mortgage is now $250,000 with teenagers who are closer to independence, your cover may now be higher than you actually need — meaning you could be overpaying. Conversely, if you have since taken on a larger mortgage, had more children, or your income has grown considerably, your existing cover may now be insufficient to actually replace your income or clear debts if something happened to you.

The point of a review is not to assume more or less cover is automatically right — it is to recalculate based on your current numbers rather than numbers from years ago.

## What income protection actually covers

Income protection insurance replaces a portion of your income (typically 62.5% to 75%, since NZ has no compulsory income protection scheme like some countries) if you are unable to work due to illness or injury. This is distinct from ACC, which covers injury but not most illness, and distinct from life insurance, which only pays out on death.

As you age, the statistical likelihood of needing income protection due to illness rises, which is exactly when many people also start to feel premiums are "expensive for something I might not use." This tension is worth thinking through deliberately rather than simply lapsing cover because the premium increased at a routine age-based review.

## The trap of relying solely on employer group cover

Many employers offer group life insurance or income protection as part of a benefits package, sometimes without individual health underwriting, which is genuinely valuable. The risk is treating this as your only cover: group policies typically end when your employment ends, which means a redundancy or career change (see our related article on navigating that) can leave you suddenly uninsured at the exact moment your life is already in flux. If group cover is your main protection, it is worth checking what portability options exist, or maintaining a smaller individual policy alongside it as a floor.

## Total risk protection, not policy-by-policy

A common mistake is reviewing each policy in isolation — checking if the life insurance premium seems reasonable, separately checking if income protection seems reasonable — rather than looking at total protection across your whole situation. A proper review asks: if I died tomorrow, would my family's mortgage be cleared and income needs met for a reasonable transition period? If I could not work for a year due to illness, could my household actually cover expenses? Answering these questions holistically, ideally with a financial adviser, produces a more sensible total cover level than adjusting individual policies piecemeal.

## Trauma and critical illness cover

Trauma (critical illness) cover pays a lump sum on diagnosis of specified serious conditions, regardless of whether you can still work. This becomes more relevant statistically in your 40s and 50s, and is worth understanding as a distinct product from income protection — it can cover costs income protection does not, like one-off treatment expenses or lifestyle adjustments, without needing to prove inability to work.

## Practical review steps

- Gather every existing policy (personal and employer-provided) and list the cover type, amount, and premium
- Recalculate what you would actually need today: outstanding mortgage, ongoing income replacement period, and dependants' needs
- Compare that to your current total cover and identify gaps or clear excesses
- Get quotes for adjusted cover before cancelling anything, since new health underwriting at an older age can sometimes mean higher premiums even for less cover — timing the transition matters

A mid-life insurance review is rarely urgent in the way a health scare or redundancy feels urgent, which is exactly why it tends to get deferred. Treating it as a scheduled five-year check-in, rather than waiting for a trigger event, tends to produce better outcomes.
