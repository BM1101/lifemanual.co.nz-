Reaching your 40s and looking at a KiwiSaver balance that feels smaller than it should be is one of the most common financial moments of this decade. The good news is that with 20 to 25 working years still ahead, there is real room to change the trajectory — but it requires a few deliberate decisions rather than hoping it sorts itself out.

## Why balances fall behind

A handful of common patterns cause this: years spent contributing at the minimum 3% rate, time out of the workforce for parenting or study, periods of self-employment where contributions lapsed entirely, or simply being in a conservative fund for years when a growth fund would have compounded far more. None of these are unusual, and none of them are unfixable — but they do need to be named before they can be addressed.

## Step one — check your contribution rate

Employee contribution rates in NZ are 3%, 4%, 6%, 8%, or 10% of gross pay. If you have been sitting at 3% for years, moving to 6% or 8% is the single biggest lever you have. On a $80,000 salary, the difference between 3% and 8% is an extra $4,000 a year going into your account — before any employer or government top-up.

If cashflow is tight, even a single step up (3% to 4%, or 4% to 6%) is worth doing now and revisiting at your next pay review.

## Step two — make sure you are getting the full employer match and government contribution

Most NZ employers match your contribution at 3% of your gross salary, provided you are contributing at least 3% yourself. If you are on a lower voluntary rate or have opted out at any point, you may be leaving employer money on the table entirely.

Separately, the government contributes up to $521.43 per year (as of the current settings) if you contribute at least $1,042.86 yourself between 1 July and 30 June. This is essentially a guaranteed return and is one of the easiest wins available — check your provider's account each June to make sure you have hit the threshold.

## Step three — review your fund type

Many people were auto-enrolled into a conservative or balanced default fund years ago and have never revisited it. With 20-plus years until retirement, a growth or aggressive fund has historically delivered meaningfully higher long-term returns than a conservative one, despite more short-term ups and downs. Being in the wrong fund for your time horizon is one of the quietest ways a balance underperforms — quiet because there is no single bad year to point to, just a slow accumulation of missed growth.

This does not mean chasing the fund with last year's best return. It means matching your fund type to how many years you actually have left before you need the money.

## Step four — consider a lump sum or short-term rate bump

If you receive a bonus, inheritance, or have paid off a smaller debt, a lump-sum top-up (up to the annual threshold for government contribution purposes) can meaningfully shift your trajectory. Alternatively, a temporary bump to a higher contribution rate for a year or two — even if you scale back afterward — adds a chunk of principal that then keeps compounding for decades.

## What realistic catch-up looks like

A worked example: someone aged 42 with a $35,000 balance, moving from 3% to 8% contributions on an $80,000 salary, with employer matching, could realistically be looking at a six-figure balance well before standard retirement age — not through investment luck, but through the mechanical effect of contributing more, for longer, in an appropriately growth-oriented fund.

The honest message is that starting to fix this at 40-something is not too late. The mistake to avoid is treating a modest balance as a reason to disengage further — it is exactly the opposite signal.

## When to get advice

If your situation includes self-employment, multiple jobs, overseas pension transfers, or you are trying to plan a specific retirement age and income, a session with a licensed financial adviser is worth the cost. This article covers general mechanics, not a plan tailored to your numbers.
