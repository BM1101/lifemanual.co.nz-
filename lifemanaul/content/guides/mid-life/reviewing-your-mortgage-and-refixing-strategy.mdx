If you bought your first home in your late 20s or early 30s, by your 40s you are typically somewhere in the middle third of your mortgage — enough equity built up to have real options, but often still 10 to 20 years of repayments ahead. This is a good point to actively review the loan rather than simply refixing on autopilot every time your term ends.

## Why this decade is different

Two things usually change between your first mortgage and your 40s: your income has likely grown, and your equity has likely grown (through repayments and, in many periods, capital gain). Both of those things open up decisions that were not available to you as a first-time buyer stretching to the edge of what you could borrow.

## Shortening the term

If your original mortgage was a 30-year term, check what happens if you shorten it to 20 or 15 years at your next refix. Because you are further into the loan and your income is often higher relative to the balance, this is frequently more affordable than people expect — and the interest savings from cutting years off the back end of a mortgage are large, since those are the years where interest would otherwise keep compounding on a shrinking but still substantial balance.

## Shopping your rate at every refix

Refixing with your existing bank because it is easy is one of the most common ways NZ homeowners overpay. Banks compete hardest for new-to-bank customers, which means loyalty is rarely rewarded with the best rate. At each refix date:

- Get a written offer from your current bank
- Get comparison rates from at least one broker or two other banks
- Ask your existing bank to match or beat the alternative before signing — most will negotiate rather than lose the account

A 0.3 to 0.5 percentage point difference sounds small but is meaningful money on a six-figure balance over a multi-year fixed term.

## Splitting fixed and floating

If you have built up savings or expect a lump sum (bonus, inheritance, business proceeds), consider putting a portion of your mortgage on a floating or short-fixed rate so you can make lump-sum repayments without break fees. Break fees on a fixed portion can be substantial if rates have moved since you fixed, so this structuring decision matters more than it did when your balance was smaller.

## Offset and revolving credit facilities

Some banks offer offset mortgages, where savings sitting in a linked account reduce the interest charged on the mortgage balance without you having to actually pay down the loan. This becomes genuinely useful once you are carrying meaningful savings — money that would otherwise sit earning modest interest (and being taxed on that interest) can instead reduce a higher-rate mortgage cost tax-free.

## Should you refinance to another bank entirely?

Full refinancing to a new lender makes sense when the rate or cashback offer materially outweighs the hassle and any break costs, or when your current bank will not move on service or rate despite repeated requests. It is generally not worth it for a marginal rate difference given the paperwork, valuation, and legal costs involved — but it is a legitimate lever if the gap is significant.

## The bigger picture

A mortgage review in your 40s is not just about the interest rate — it is a chance to decide, deliberately, whether you are optimising for paying the house off faster, freeing up cashflow for other goals like KiwiSaver catch-up, or building a buffer for a future renovation or downsizing decision. The right answer depends on your wider financial picture, not the mortgage in isolation.
