Parents in mid-life are often simultaneously trying to get their own finances in order and trying to figure out how to actually teach a teenager about money — a subject most adults never had formally taught to them either. The good news is that the research on this is fairly consistent: modelling and real experience teach far more than lectures do.

## Teenagers learn from what they see, not what they are told

Studies on financial socialisation consistently find that children and teenagers absorb far more from observing how their parents talk about and handle money — arguing about bills, discussing a purchase decision out loud, showing visible stress or calm around spending — than from any deliberate "money talk." This does not mean narrating every financial decision in detail, but it does mean that occasionally including a teenager in an age-appropriate real decision (comparing insurance quotes, deciding whether a purchase is worth it) teaches more than a one-off lecture.

## Give real control over a small, real amount

A common instinct is to manage a teenager's money closely to prevent mistakes. The more effective approach, according to most financial literacy research, is closer to the opposite: giving a teenager a defined, real amount of money (an allowance, part-time job earnings, or a set clothing/spending budget) and letting them make their own decisions with it — including bad ones. A teenager who blows a whole allowance on something they regret in week one learns a lesson that no amount of parental warning would have taught as effectively, provided the stakes are genuinely small enough to absorb.

## Opening their own bank account

Most NZ banks offer youth accounts with no or low fees, and getting a teenager involved in opening their own account — filling in forms, understanding what a bank card is, seeing a balance change after a deposit — demystifies a system that many adults still find intimidating because they never had a hands-on introduction. This is also a natural point to introduce basic concepts like the difference between savings and everyday accounts, and what interest actually means in practice.

## First jobs and the first payslip

A first part-time job is often the first time a teenager encounters PAYE tax, KiwiSaver deductions, and the gap between gross and net pay. Walking through a first payslip together — what each line means, why the number in the bank is smaller than the hourly rate times hours worked — turns an abstract future concept into something concrete and immediately relevant.

## Introducing KiwiSaver early

Teenagers with part-time jobs are often eligible to join KiwiSaver, and while it is a genuinely long-term decision, introducing the concept of compounding and long-term saving using their own small KiwiSaver balance as a live example makes the concept far more real than discussing it in the abstract. This is also a natural opening to explain, briefly, why you are reviewing your own KiwiSaver settings in your 40s (see our related article) — modelling active engagement rather than treating it as something you set once and ignore.

## Talking about debt before it is urgent

Discussing credit cards, buy-now-pay-later schemes, and eventually student loans before a teenager is actually applying for one gives them a chance to absorb the concept without the pressure of an immediate decision. Buy-now-pay-later services in particular are heavily marketed to younger users and worth discussing specifically and directly, since many teenagers encounter them online well before any formal financial education would otherwise cover them.

## What not to do

Avoid using money as a purely punitive or reward-based tool disconnected from actual financial lessons (for example, docking allowance for unrelated behaviour issues), since this tends to muddy the specific lesson about money management. Similarly, bailing a teenager out immediately every time they overspend removes the natural consequence that is often the most effective teacher — within limits that keep the stakes genuinely safe.
