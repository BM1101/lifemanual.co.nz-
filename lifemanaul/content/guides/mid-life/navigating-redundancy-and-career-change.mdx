Redundancy or a forced career change lands differently in your 40s or 50s than it did earlier in your working life. Financial commitments are usually larger, and there can be a quiet worry about ageism in hiring — but there is also, often overlooked, considerably more leverage than people expect, built from years of experience and a clearer sense of what you actually want from work.

## Your legal position if you are made redundant

Redundancy in NZ must be genuine — the role itself must no longer be needed for legitimate business reasons, not used as a way to remove a specific person without following a proper performance process. Employers are required to:

- Consult with you before a final decision is made, not present redundancy as already decided
- Explain the business reasons and give you a genuine opportunity to respond
- Consider reasonable alternatives, including redeployment to another role if one exists

If a redundancy process skips consultation or looks like it was really about performance or personal issues, it may be able to be challenged as unjustified dismissal through a personal grievance.

## Redundancy compensation is not automatic

A common misconception is that redundancy pay is a legal right in NZ. It is not — unless your employment agreement specifically provides for a redundancy payment, there is no default statutory entitlement (aside from your normal final pay, accrued leave, and any notice period owed). Check your employment agreement carefully; some do include a formula, often based on length of service, and this is worth confirming rather than assuming.

## Immediate practical steps

- Request the redundancy proposal and any supporting information in writing
- Check your employment agreement for a notice period, redundancy clause, and restraint of trade clauses that might limit your next role
- Register with Work and Income promptly if there will be a gap in income — timing matters for some payments
- Consider a free or low-cost session with an employment lawyer or advocate if anything about the process feels rushed or unclear, particularly before signing any settlement agreement

## Reframing a mid-life career pivot

Separately from redundancy, many people in their 40s and 50s choose to pivot deliberately — moving from a corporate role to something more flexible, changing industries entirely, or reducing hours in exchange for more control over their time. The instinct to assume this is riskier than staying put is not always accurate. Two decades of transferable skills — managing people, running projects, understanding a customer or an industry — are more portable than they were at 25, even into a different sector.

## Retraining and support options

- Study Link and targeted funding for retraining are worth checking, since some government support exists specifically for people changing careers, not only school leavers
- Industry-specific bridging courses can shorten the time to become credible in a new field, compared to starting entirely from scratch
- Contract or consulting work in your existing field can bridge income while you build capability in a new direction, rather than making an all-or-nothing leap

## Addressing the age question directly

Concerns about ageism in hiring are not imaginary, but they are also not the whole picture. Framing your experience around outcomes and adaptability, rather than years served, tends to land better than either downplaying your experience or leading with tenure as the main selling point. Networks built over a longer career are also a genuine advantage at this stage — many mid-life role changes happen through personal connections rather than cold applications.

## The financial buffer question

Whether facing redundancy or choosing a pivot, having 3 to 6 months of essential expenses set aside changes the calculus considerably — it turns a forced, fast decision into a more considered one. If you do not currently have that buffer, it is worth prioritising before making a voluntary change, even if it means deferring the pivot by a few months.
