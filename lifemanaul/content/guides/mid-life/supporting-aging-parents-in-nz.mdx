For many people, the first sign that parents need more support is small — a missed bill, a fall, a bit more confusion on the phone than usual. What follows can either be a manageable, planned transition or a stressful scramble, and the difference usually comes down to whether the groundwork was laid in advance.

## Start with enduring power of attorney

An Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA) lets your parent appoint someone — often you or a sibling — to make decisions on their behalf if they lose the capacity to make those decisions themselves, covering both property/finances and personal care and welfare. Critically, an EPA has to be set up while your parent still has legal capacity to understand and sign it. Once capacity is lost, the only remaining option is applying to the Family Court for a welfare guardianship or property order, which is slower, more expensive, and more stressful.

Raising this while your parents are well is far easier than raising it during a hospital admission. Framing it as "sorting out paperwork now so nobody has to guess later" tends to land better than framing it around decline.

## Know the two main entry points to support

**Work and Income** administers financial support, including the Residential Care Subsidy application process and various supplementary assistance for older people.

**Needs Assessment Service Coordination (NASC)** is the health system's entry point for assessing what support an older person needs — home help, respite care, or residential care — and connecting them to funded services. A referral usually comes through a GP, but families can also request an assessment directly. Most people have never heard of NASC until they need it, and knowing it exists before a crisis saves valuable time when one does happen.

## Understand how residential care costs actually work

If a parent needs long-term residential care, costs are means-tested through a formal financial means assessment, which looks at both income and assets. The family home is generally exempt from the assets test if a spouse or certain dependent relatives still live in it, but the rules have specific conditions and thresholds that change periodically — this is an area worth getting a proper Work and Income or specialist assessment on rather than relying on general assumptions, since getting it wrong can affect eligibility for subsidised care.

## The financial conversation

Many adult children do not know their parents' financial position until a crisis forces disclosure. A calm, early conversation about where relevant documents are kept (will, EPA, bank details, insurance policies), whether there is a financial adviser or accountant already involved, and what your parents' wishes are around care versus staying at home, removes a huge amount of guesswork later. This does not require detailed numbers — just knowing where to look and who to call.

## The sandwich generation reality

If you are also raising children while supporting aging parents, the financial and emotional load compounds. This is common enough in your 40s and 50s to have a name for a reason. Practical steps that help: involving siblings explicitly in a shared plan rather than one person absorbing everything by default, looking into respite care options even if you feel you should be able to manage without them, and being honest with your own family about the trade-offs — something has to give, and deciding deliberately what that is beats it being decided by exhaustion.

## When to bring in professionals

An elder law specialist, a financial adviser familiar with means-testing, or a geriatrician for a proper needs assessment are all worth the cost when the situation is complex — blended families, disputed capacity, or significant assets. For most families, though, the highest-value step is simply starting the EPA and NASC conversations early, while there is no urgency forcing the pace.
