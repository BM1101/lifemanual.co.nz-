Midlife brings physical changes that are normal, well documented, and still routinely under-discussed — which means many people spend months or years attributing symptoms to stress, work, or "just getting older" before realising there is a specific, addressable cause.

## What perimenopause actually is

Perimenopause is the transition period leading up to menopause (the point when periods stop for 12 consecutive months), driven by fluctuating hormone levels — primarily oestrogen and progesterone. It typically starts in the mid-to-late 40s but can begin earlier, and can last anywhere from a few months to several years before periods stop entirely.

Common symptoms include irregular periods, hot flushes and night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, brain fog, and joint aches. Because these symptoms are broad and can overlap with normal life stress, they are frequently dismissed — by workplaces, by partners, and sometimes by the person experiencing them — as something else entirely.

## Why this often goes unaddressed for longer than it should

Many people do not realise perimenopause can start well before periods become noticeably irregular, so they do not connect symptoms like mood changes or disrupted sleep to a hormonal cause. There is also, still, a degree of reluctance to bring the topic up directly with a GP, particularly in a short appointment slot. Being specific and direct — naming perimenopause as a possibility rather than describing only individual symptoms — tends to get a more useful conversation started.

## Options worth discussing with a GP

Treatment options range considerably in intensity, and what is appropriate depends heavily on individual history, symptoms, and risk factors — this is a conversation for a GP or specialist, not a general guide. Broadly, options that get discussed include hormone replacement therapy (HRT), non-hormonal medications for specific symptoms, and lifestyle adjustments. The key practical point is that effective options exist and this does not need to simply be endured — but reaching them usually requires actively raising the topic rather than waiting for it to be raised.

## Midlife changes are not only a female experience

Men also experience a gradual decline in testosterone through midlife, sometimes referred to informally as andropause, though it is more gradual and less sharply defined than female perimenopause. Symptoms can include fatigue, reduced motivation, mood changes, and changes in body composition. These are also worth raising with a GP rather than assuming they are simply an unavoidable part of aging — some causes are treatable, and some symptoms overlap with other conditions worth ruling out.

## Screening worth booking in your 40s and 50s

A number of screening checks become relevant in this decade, several of which are free or subsidised in NZ:

- Free breast screening (mammograms) through BreastScreen Aotearoa from age 45 (with earlier access from 40 on request)
- Free bowel screening through the National Bowel Screening Programme from age 60, with earlier access for those with a family history or symptoms
- Cervical screening continues to be relevant and has moved to a self-testing option for many people
- Cardiovascular risk assessments through your GP, particularly if there is a family history of heart disease
- Blood pressure and cholesterol checks, which become more worth doing routinely from your 40s onward

Many of these require you to book proactively rather than being automatically reminded, so it is worth putting a recurring reminder in your calendar rather than relying on the system to prompt you.

## The general point

Midlife health changes are common, well understood by the medical system even if under-discussed socially, and in most cases genuinely manageable once named. The main practical barrier is usually not a lack of available support — it is not raising the topic clearly enough for that support to be offered.
