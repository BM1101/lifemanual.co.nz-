Somewhere in mid-life, many homeowners hit a moment where the family home no longer quite fits — children have left or are about to, the house needs work, or priorities around location and lifestyle have shifted. The choice between renovating and downsizing is rarely obvious, and it is worth working through deliberately rather than defaulting to whichever option feels less disruptive in the moment.

## Start with what has actually changed

Two different problems get conflated here, and they point toward different answers:

**Your needs have changed** — fewer people living in the house, wanting to be closer to town or family, wanting less maintenance. This points toward considering a move, since no amount of renovation changes location or fundamentally reduces the size of a property.

**Your house has changed** — it is aging, poorly laid out for how you now live, or needs significant work regardless of who lives there. This points toward considering renovation, since a move would just relocate the same underlying preferences to a different property that may have its own issues.

Being honest about which problem you are actually solving avoids the common trap of renovating a house whose location no longer suits you, or moving away from a location you love because of a fixable house problem.

## The real cost of renovating

Renovation budgets are consistently underestimated, for reasons that are well documented across the industry: unexpected issues once walls or floors are opened up, materials cost changes between quoting and building, and scope creep once a renovation is underway and "while we're at it" additions start to seem reasonable. Standard advice from quantity surveyors and builders is to add a contingency of at least 15 to 20% on top of any quoted renovation budget — and to actually hold that money aside rather than treating the base quote as the real number.

Getting at least two to three detailed quotes, checking references and past work, and understanding what is and is not included in a quote (site works, consents, fixtures) prevents a large share of renovation cost blowouts.

## The real financial picture of downsizing

Downsizing is often assumed to straightforwardly free up a large sum of money, but the actual maths is less generous once you account for real estate agent commission (typically a few percent of sale price), legal fees on both the sale and purchase, moving costs, and the fact that a smaller property in the same desirable area may not actually cost dramatically less than the family home. Downsizing to a different, cheaper region can free up more capital than downsizing within the same area — worth modelling both scenarios with real numbers before assuming the outcome.

## How this connects to your mortgage and retirement timeline

This decision does not sit in isolation. If you are actively working through a mortgage refixing strategy (see our related article), the state of your existing loan affects what a renovation or move actually costs — break fees, available equity, and serviceability for any new borrowing all matter. Similarly, if downsizing is partly motivated by wanting to free up capital for retirement, it is worth modelling that alongside your KiwiSaver and other retirement savings, rather than treating housing and retirement planning as two separate conversations.

## Questions worth answering before deciding

- If I renovate, will the house still suit me in another ten years, or am I solving a temporary problem?
- If I downsize, have I actually run the numbers on transaction costs, not just the difference in sale price versus purchase price?
- Am I solving a location problem, a size problem, or a house-condition problem — and does my preferred option actually address that specific problem?
- What does each option do to my mortgage position and timeline to being debt-free?

There is no universally right answer here — it depends heavily on individual circumstances, local property market conditions, and what you are actually trying to achieve. But working through it with real numbers, rather than a general feeling that "we should probably downsize" or "we should probably renovate," leads to noticeably better decisions.
