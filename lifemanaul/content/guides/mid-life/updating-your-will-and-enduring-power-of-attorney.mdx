If your will was written before you had children, before a second relationship, or before you owned significant property, it is worth treating a mid-life review as a routine task rather than something to get around to eventually. Wills do not update themselves, and the gap between what a will says and what someone would actually want can be significant after a decade or two of life changes.

## Marriage and relationship changes can invalidate an old will

Under NZ law, marriage generally revokes a prior will unless that will was explicitly made in contemplation of the marriage. Many people are surprised to learn this — meaning a will written while single, before later marrying, may have no legal effect at all despite still sitting in a drawer somewhere. Entering a civil union has a similar effect. Separation (without formal divorce) does not automatically revoke a will, which is its own trap if you have separated but not yet finalised anything legally.

## Guardianship nominations for children

If you have children, your will is the place to nominate a testamentary guardian — someone who would share guardianship responsibilities if both parents died. Many parents assume "of course my sister would take the kids" without ever writing it down, which leaves the decision open to family disagreement or a court process at the worst possible time. This is a short clause to add and one of the highest-value updates a mid-life will review can make.

## Blended families need explicit provisions

If you have stepchildren, a second marriage, or children from more than one relationship, a generic "everything to my spouse" will can produce outcomes you would not have chosen — for instance, your biological children potentially receiving nothing if your spouse remarries after your death and their new will favours a new family. Blended family situations are exactly where specific, carefully drafted provisions matter most, and where a template or DIY will is riskiest.

## Asset changes since your last will

A will written when your main asset was a car and a small KiwiSaver balance may not sensibly address a family home, a business, an investment property, or a larger KiwiSaver and insurance payout. Specific bequests that made sense with a smaller estate can become impractical or unfair at a larger scale. A mid-life review is the moment to check that the will's structure still matches what you actually own.

## Your own Enduring Power of Attorney

It is easy to think of Enduring Powers of Attorney as something you help your parents set up, while forgetting to set up your own. An EPA appoints someone to make property and welfare decisions on your behalf if you are ever unable to — through accident, illness, or incapacity at any age, not just old age. Without one, your family may need to apply to the Family Court to get authority to act for you, which is slower and more stressful than having it sorted in advance.

## Practical next steps

- Locate your existing will and EPA (if any) and check the date and circumstances they were made under
- List major life changes since then: marriage, divorce, children, stepchildren, property, business ownership
- Book a session with a lawyer for a proper review rather than a DIY update, particularly if your family situation is blended or your assets are more than straightforward
- Set a reminder to revisit both documents again in five years, or sooner after any major life event

A will and EPA review does not need to be dramatic or morbid — it is closer to updating your emergency contacts or beneficiary nominations: routine admin that matters a great deal exactly when you are not there to explain your intentions.
