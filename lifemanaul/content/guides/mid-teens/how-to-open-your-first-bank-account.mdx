A bank account is one of the first genuinely adult things you need. Without one you cannot get paid, pay for things online, or do much of anything in the modern financial system. The good news is that opening one is straightforward, and most NZ banks make it easy for young people.

## What age can you open an account?

Most NZ banks will let you open a basic account from age 13 with a parent or guardian present and signing. From age 16, you can generally open an account in your own name without parental involvement — though exact rules vary by bank.

If you are under 18, ask specifically about youth or student accounts. These typically have no monthly fees, which is exactly what you want.

## What you will need to bring

Before you go to a branch or start an online application, gather these:

**Photo ID** — a passport is best. If you do not have one, a birth certificate plus another form of ID (school ID, community services card) usually works. A driver licence works once you have one.

**Proof of address** — a letter addressed to you at your home address. A bank statement, utility bill, or letter from school usually works. If you live with your parents, a letter from them confirming your address is generally accepted.

**IRD number** — your tax number. If you do not have one yet, you can still open the account — you just need to apply separately. See our guide on getting your IRD number.

**A parent or guardian** — required if you are under 16. They will need their own ID too.

## Which bank should you choose?

The five main retail banks in NZ are ANZ, ASB, BNZ, Kiwibank, and Westpac. For a first account, the differences between them are small. More important questions:

**Is there a branch near you?** Useful for when you need in-person help, which you occasionally will.

**How is the app?** You will manage most of your banking on your phone. Download a couple and see which feels most intuitive.

**Are there fees?** For under-18s, most banks offer completely fee-free accounts. Confirm this before signing up. If anyone tries to put you on a fee-charging account, ask about the youth option.

**Is it Kiwibank?** Kiwibank is NZ-owned (majority owned by the NZ government), which some people prefer. It is a solid option with competitive youth accounts.

## Transaction account vs savings account

You want two accounts, not one:

**Transaction account** (also called an everyday or cheque account) — this is where your money lives day-to-day. Your pay goes in, your spending comes out. It comes with an EFTPOS or debit card.

**Savings account** — a separate account for money you are not planning to spend. Typically earns a small amount of interest. You usually do not get a card for it, which is intentional — the friction of having to transfer money makes you less likely to dip into it.

Most banks let you open both at the same time. Do it. Even if you start with zero in the savings account, having it there means you can start building the habit.

## Opening online vs in-branch

Most NZ banks now let you open an account entirely online or through their app. This is faster and works well if you have a NZ passport — the app can scan it. If you are using a birth certificate or have a more complicated situation, going into a branch in person is easier.

## Things to watch out for

**Monthly fees** — some accounts charge $5 to $15 per month. At your stage, you should pay nothing. If a bank tries to put you on a fee-charging account, ask about youth options.

**Overdraft** — some accounts automatically approve small overdrafts (letting you go below zero). This can seem useful but it is essentially a mini-loan at a high interest rate. Ask to have overdraft disabled if you want to avoid the temptation.

**Savings interest rates** — most savings accounts pay very low interest (often 0.5 to 2%). This is fine for now. The point at this stage is building the habit of saving, not maximising returns. That comes later.

## After you open your account

Once your account is open:

1. Set up internet banking — download the app and create your login
2. Note your account number — you will need this to give to employers
3. Activate your card — usually done in the app or at an ATM
4. Consider setting up an automatic transfer — even $10 a week moving to your savings account builds a habit that pays off over time

That is it. You are in the financial system. The next step is learning how to manage what flows through it — see our guide on budgeting your first income.
