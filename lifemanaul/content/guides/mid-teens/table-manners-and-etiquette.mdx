Table manners might sound old-fashioned, but they matter in ways that are very practical. How you behave at a dinner table — with a family, at a job interview lunch, on a date, at a business meeting — communicates whether you are considerate of the people around you. That impression follows you.

## The basics that cover almost everything

These apply in virtually every dining situation:

**Wait for everyone to be served before eating** — or for the host to indicate it is okay to start. Starting before others are seated or served is one of the most universally noticed breaches of etiquette.

**Chew with your mouth closed** — sounds obvious, but worth being conscious of.

**No phones at the table** — or if your phone is out, it is face down and you are not checking it. Pulling out your phone during a meal signals that whatever is on the screen is more important than the people you are with.

**Do not talk with your mouth full** — finish what you are chewing, then speak.

**Use utensils, not your hands** — with exceptions for things clearly designed to be eaten by hand (bread, some shared appetisers).

**Do not reach across people** — ask for things to be passed.

**Pace yourself with the group** — try to finish at roughly the same time as others at the table. Eating very fast or very slowly both draw attention.

**Say please and thank you** — to the host, to staff at restaurants, to whoever served you.

## At a restaurant

**Ordering:** Know what you want before the server arrives. If you are not ready, it is fine to say so — but try to be decisive. Do not order the most or least expensive thing on the menu if someone else is paying.

**Sending food back:** If something is genuinely wrong (undercooked, wrong dish), it is fine to send it back politely. If you just do not like it, eat what you can and do not make a scene.

**Splitting the bill:** If someone has invited you out, they may intend to pay — let them, and say thank you genuinely. If it is a group of peers, the clearest approach is to split equally unless someone has ordered significantly more.

**Tipping:** NZ does not have a strong tipping culture like the US. Tipping is appreciated but not expected. If the service was good and you want to tip, 10% is generous.

## Formal dining

If you ever find yourself at a formal dinner — a wedding, a corporate event, a formal interview meal — a few extra things apply:

**Cutlery:** Work from the outside in. The cutlery furthest from your plate is for the first course. If you are not sure, watch what the host does.

**Bread:** Tear bread with your hands, do not cut it. Butter only a small piece at a time, not the whole roll.

**Glasses:** Your bread plate is on the left, your drinks are on the right. A useful memory trick: BMW — bread, meal, water, left to right.

**Napkin:** Goes in your lap when you sit down. If you need to leave the table briefly, leave it folded on your chair. At the end of the meal, place it loosely folded to the left of your plate.

**Posture:** Sit up reasonably straight. Elbows off the table while eating (though this rule is relaxed between courses).

**Conversation:** At formal dinners, converse with the people on both sides of you. Do not monopolise one person. Avoid controversial topics (politics, religion) unless you know the group well.

## When you are the guest

If someone has cooked for you:

- Compliment the food, even if it is not your favourite
- Offer to help clear up
- Thank them genuinely before you leave
- Do not criticise the food, the cooking, or the choices

## When you are hosting

If you are serving food to guests:

- Make sure everyone has what they need before you sit down
- Indicate when it is okay to start eating
- Keep the conversation moving — your job as host is to make guests comfortable

## Why this matters

Etiquette is not about performing for appearances. It is about signalling that you are aware of and considerate of the people around you. The specific rules vary across cultures and contexts — what matters is the underlying mindset of thinking about others, not just yourself.

People who are comfortable in social situations — who seem naturally at ease at dinners, events, and meetings — are not necessarily born that way. They have learned a basic repertoire of behaviour and practised it. It becomes second nature quickly.
