Getting your first pay is exciting. Spending it all immediately is extremely easy. Building the habit of managing money well from the start is one of the most valuable things you can do — because the habits you form now tend to stick.

This guide gives you a simple system that works whether you are earning $50 a week from a part-time job or $500.

## What is a budget?

A budget is simply a plan for where your money goes. It is not about restriction — it is about intention. Without a budget, money tends to disappear in ways you cannot quite account for. With one, you decide in advance where it goes.

## The 50/30/20 rule

This is one of the most widely used budgeting frameworks because it is simple and flexible:

- **50% on needs** — things you genuinely need (food, transport to work or school, essential clothing)
- **30% on wants** — things you enjoy but do not strictly need (eating out, entertainment, clothes beyond basics)
- **20% on saving** — money you set aside and do not spend

At your age, you probably do not have many fixed expenses (rent, bills), so your "needs" category might be quite small. That is fine — adjust the percentages to fit your situation. The key principle is that saving is not optional. It comes off the top, not after everything else.

## Pay yourself first

The single most effective budgeting habit is this: when you get paid, immediately move your savings amount into your savings account before you do anything else.

Most people try to save what is left over at the end of the month. There is almost never anything left over. If you move the money first, you adjust your spending to whatever remains — rather than spending everything and saving nothing.

Even $20 a pay cycle, moved automatically the moment your pay comes in, builds a habit and a balance over time.

## A simple system

You do not need complicated spreadsheets. Here is a basic setup:

**Two accounts** — a transaction account for day-to-day spending, and a savings account you treat as untouchable. Every time you get paid, move your savings amount immediately.

**One weekly check-in** — spend five minutes once a week looking at what you have spent. Most banking apps show your spending broken down by category. You are just looking to see whether your spending matches your intentions. No judgment required — just awareness.

**One question before buying something** — "do I actually want this, or am I just bored or impulse buying?" This single pause prevents a significant amount of regret.

## Tracking your spending

Most people have no idea what they actually spend money on. The categories that tend to surprise people most: food and drinks (coffees, lunches, snacks add up much faster than expected), subscriptions (things you signed up for and forgot about), and online shopping.

Your bank app is the easiest tracking tool. Most modern banking apps automatically categorise your spending. Look at last month's statement and see where your money went. This is often the most motivating thing you can do — once you see it, it is hard to unsee.

## A note on buy now, pay later

Services like Afterpay and Laybuy let you buy something and pay for it in instalments. They are fine if you use them for things you were already going to buy and can genuinely afford — but they make it easy to spend money you do not have on things you do not need.

The problem is that multiple BNPL commitments stack up invisibly. You might have several running at once without a clear picture of what is coming out of your account each fortnight. If you use them, keep it to one at a time and only for planned purchases.

## Goals make saving easier

Saving "for the future" in the abstract is hard. Saving for a specific thing — a trip, a laptop, a car — is much easier because you can see the progress and the purpose.

Decide on one near-term savings goal and track progress toward it. Even $10 a week toward a $500 goal is visible momentum.

## What good money habits look like

The goal is not perfection — it is a general direction:

- You know roughly what you earn and what you spend
- Some money automatically goes to savings before you can spend it
- You are not regularly spending more than you earn
- You have a small buffer for unexpected costs

That is it. You do not need to be particularly disciplined or organised — you just need a system simple enough that it runs mostly on autopilot. The habits built now become the foundation for managing much larger amounts of money later.
