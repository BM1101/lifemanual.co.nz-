Consent is one of those topics that often gets taught in a way that feels clinical or uncomfortable. This guide treats it the way it deserves — as something genuinely important, explained plainly.

## What consent actually means

Consent is an agreement between people to participate in an activity together. In the context of physical or sexual activity, consent means:

**Freely given** — not because of pressure, manipulation, or fear. A yes that comes from "I feel like I have to" is not genuine consent.

**Reversible** — anyone can change their mind at any time, even if they said yes before, even if you are already in the middle of something. Changing your mind is always okay.

**Informed** — both people know what they are agreeing to.

**Enthusiastic** — genuine consent is not reluctant. If someone seems unsure, hesitant, or like they are going along with something rather than actually wanting it, that is not a clear yes.

**Specific** — saying yes to one thing does not mean yes to everything. Agreeing to kiss someone does not mean agreeing to anything more.

A useful way to think about it: consent is an enthusiastic yes, not the absence of a no.

## What consent is not

- Silence
- Not saying no
- Going along with something without comment
- Agreement given while drunk or high
- Agreement given under pressure or manipulation
- A previous yes (including from a past encounter or relationship)

## Asking for consent

Asking for consent does not have to be awkward. It sounds like:

- "Is this okay?"
- "Do you want to?"
- "Are you comfortable with this?"
- "I want to — do you?"

Pay attention to the response — both the words and the body language. If someone seems hesitant, unsure, or uncomfortable, stop and check in. An enthusiastic yes looks different from a reluctant yes.

## Giving and withdrawing consent

You are always allowed to say no. You are always allowed to change your mind. You never owe anyone sexual contact regardless of what has happened before, what they have done for you, or how long you have been together.

If you want to stop something:

- "I want to stop."
- "I'm not comfortable with this."
- "Can we not do this?"

If someone does not stop when you ask them to, that is a serious problem and you should tell a trusted adult or contact a support service.

## Consent and alcohol

Alcohol affects judgment and the ability to make informed decisions. The legal standard in NZ is that someone who is intoxicated cannot give meaningful consent. If someone is drunk, they cannot properly consent — and having sex with someone who is significantly intoxicated is sexual assault, regardless of what they seemed to agree to at the time.

This applies to you too. If you are drunk, any decisions you make about sexual activity are made with impaired judgment. It is worth being aware of this.

## Age of consent in New Zealand

The age of consent in New Zealand is 16. This means that sexual activity between two people where one or both are under 16 is illegal, regardless of whether both people agreed to it.

There is a close-in-age exception: sexual activity between young people who are both aged 12 to 15 is not prosecuted if there is less than 2 years' age difference and it was consensual. But this is a legal technicality, not an encouragement.

## If something has happened to you

If something has happened to you without your consent — whether recently or in the past — it was not your fault. It does not matter what you were wearing, whether you had been drinking, whether you had consented previously, or whether you said no clearly enough. Non-consensual sexual activity is never the fault of the person it happened to.

Support is available:

- **Safe to Talk (sexual harm helpline)**: 0800 044 334, text 4334, or chat at safetotalk.nz — available 24/7, free and confidential
- **1737**: text or call for free counselling support
- **Your school counsellor** or a trusted adult

You do not have to report it to police if you do not want to. But talking to someone — even just to process what happened — is worth doing.

## A final note

Consent is not just a rule to follow to avoid getting in trouble. It is about treating other people as full human beings whose feelings and choices matter. Relationships — of any kind — built on genuine mutual respect feel completely different from ones where someone is going along with something reluctantly.

Getting this right is not complicated: check in, listen to the answer, and stop if someone seems uncertain or says stop.
