If someone asked you to choose between $1,000 today or a single cent that doubled every day for 30 days, what would you take?

Most people take the thousand dollars. The right answer is the penny — after 30 days of doubling, it becomes $10,737,418.24.

That's the essence of compound interest: small things grow into enormous things when you give them enough time. And it's why what you do with money in your 20s matters more than almost anything financial you'll do in your 40s.

## Simple interest vs compound interest

**Simple interest** means you earn interest only on the original amount (called the principal). Put $1,000 in an account at 7% simple interest and you earn $70 every year, forever.

**Compound interest** means you earn interest on the principal *and* on the interest you've already earned. In year one you earn $70. In year two you earn $74.90 — because you now have $1,070 earning interest. In year three, $80.14. In year ten, you have $1,967.15. You've earned $967 without adding a single extra dollar.

The difference is small at the start. It becomes staggering over decades.

## The Rule of 72

Here's a mental shortcut worth memorising: **divide 72 by your interest rate to find out how many years it takes to double your money.**

- At 4% (savings account): money doubles every 18 years
- At 7% (long-term share market average): doubles every ~10 years
- At 10% (strong market years): doubles every 7.2 years

So $10,000 invested at 18 in a fund earning 7% annually becomes:
- $20,000 by age 28
- $40,000 by age 38
- $80,000 by age 48
- $160,000 by age 58
- $320,000 by age 68

**You added nothing.** That's $310,000 of growth from $10,000 and 50 years of patience.

## Why starting early changes everything

Let's compare two people. Both invest $300 per month into an index fund earning 7% annually.

**Alex** starts at 22 and stops at 32 — just 10 years of contributions, $36,000 total invested.

**Jordan** starts at 32 and never stops — contributing every month until age 65, $126,000 total invested.

By age 65, Alex has **more money** than Jordan — despite investing less than a third as much — simply because the money had a longer runway to compound.

This is the single most important financial insight for your 20s. You don't have to invest a lot. You have to invest *early*.

<Callout>
The best time to start investing was 10 years ago. The second best time is today. Don't wait until you feel like you have enough to invest — start with whatever you can.

## Where compound interest works for you

**KiwiSaver** — New Zealand's retirement savings scheme compounds over your entire working life. Even the default contributions add up enormously if you start at 18. See our guide on KiwiSaver to understand your options.

**Index funds** — a simple investment that tracks the share market. Over long periods, share markets have returned roughly 7–10% annually before inflation. This is the engine most financial advisors recommend for long-term wealth.

**High-interest savings accounts** — while rates are lower (2–5%), they're better than nothing and your money is immediately accessible. Good for your emergency fund.

## Where compound interest works against you

The same mathematics that makes compound interest powerful for saving makes it devastating for debt.

A credit card charging 20% annually compounds just as aggressively. $5,000 of credit card debt left unpaid for 10 years becomes $30,958. This is why paying off high-interest debt is almost always the right financial move before starting to invest.

Student loans in New Zealand are interest-free while you're earning under the threshold — so those are generally lower priority than other debt.

## How to actually start

You don't need to understand investing deeply before you start. Here's the simplest possible approach:

1. **Make sure you're enrolled in KiwiSaver.** If you're working, this is the easiest win — your employer contributes on top of your own contributions, and the government adds up to $521 per year. It's free money.

2. **Open a simple investment account.** Platforms like Sharesies, InvestNow, or Kernel let you start with as little as $5 and invest in diversified index funds.

3. **Set up an automatic contribution.** Even $20 per week. The amount matters less than the habit and the time.

4. **Don't touch it.** Compound interest requires patience. The worst thing you can do is pull money out when markets dip — that's when you lock in losses and miss the recovery.

The hardest part isn't the mathematics. It's starting before you feel ready. Nobody feels ready. Start anyway.
