If someone asked you to choose between $1,000 today or a single cent that doubled every day for 30 days, what would you take?

Most people take the thousand dollars. The right answer is the penny — after 30 days of doubling, it becomes $10,737,418.24.

That is the essence of compound interest: small things grow into enormous things when you give them enough time. And it is why what you do with money in your 20s matters more than almost anything financial you will do in your 40s.

## Simple interest vs compound interest

Simple interest means you earn interest only on the original amount. Put $1,000 in an account at 7% simple interest and you earn $70 every year, forever.

Compound interest means you earn interest on the principal and on the interest you have already earned. In year one you earn $70. In year two you earn $74.90 — because you now have $1,070 earning interest. In year three, $80.14. In year ten, you have $1,967. You have earned $967 without adding a single extra dollar.

The difference is small at the start. It becomes staggering over decades.

## The Rule of 72

Here is a mental shortcut worth memorising: divide 72 by your interest rate to find out how many years it takes to double your money.

At 4% your money doubles every 18 years. At 7% every 10 years. At 10% every 7.2 years.

So $10,000 invested at 18 in a fund earning 7% annually becomes $20,000 by age 28, $40,000 by 38, $80,000 by 48, $160,000 by 58, and $320,000 by 68. You added nothing. That is $310,000 of growth from $10,000 and 50 years of patience.

## Why starting early changes everything

Switch to the "Cost of waiting" tab in the calculator above. Two people both invest the same amount every month at the same return rate. The only difference is when they start. The gap in their final balances is not the money they missed contributing — it is the compound growth those early years would have generated.

This is the single most important financial insight for your 20s. You do not have to invest a lot. You have to invest early.

## Where compound interest works for you

KiwiSaver compounds over your entire working life. Even the default contributions add up enormously if you start at 18. See our guide on how to invest for the first time to understand your options.

Index funds — simple investments that track the share market — have returned roughly 7 to 10 percent annually over long periods. This is the engine most financial advisors recommend for long-term wealth.

## Where compound interest works against you

The same mathematics that makes compound interest powerful for saving makes it devastating for debt. A credit card charging 20% annually compounds just as aggressively. $5,000 of credit card debt left unpaid for 10 years becomes $30,958. This is why paying off high-interest debt is almost always the right financial move before starting to invest.

## How to actually start

You do not need to understand investing deeply before you start. Make sure you are enrolled in KiwiSaver. Open a simple investment account on a platform like Sharesies, InvestNow, or Kernel. Set up an automatic contribution of even $20 per week. Do not touch it.

The hardest part is not the mathematics. It is starting before you feel ready. Nobody feels ready. Start anyway.
