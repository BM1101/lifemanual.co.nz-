If someone gave you the choice between $1,000 today or a cent that doubled every day for 30 days, most people would take the thousand dollars. The penny, compounded daily, becomes $10,737,418.

That is compound interest. Small things, given enough time, become very large things.

## Simple vs compound interest

**Simple interest** earns on the original amount only. $10,000 at 7% simple interest earns $700 every year, forever. After 20 years you have $24,000.

**Compound interest** earns on the original amount and on all the interest already earned. $10,000 at 7% compound interest earns $700 in year one, then $749 in year two (because you now have $10,700 earning interest), then $801 in year three. After 20 years you have $38,697 — not $24,000.

The difference seems small early on. Over decades it becomes extraordinary.

## The Rule of 72

A useful mental shortcut: divide 72 by your annual return rate to find how many years it takes to double your money.

At 6%: doubles every 12 years
At 7%: doubles every 10.3 years
At 9%: doubles every 8 years

So $10,000 invested at 7% becomes:
- $20,000 after ~10 years
- $40,000 after ~20 years
- $80,000 after ~30 years
- $160,000 after ~40 years

Without adding a single dollar after the initial investment. That is the power of time.

## Why your twenties matter more than your forties

This is the counterintuitive part: the decade in which you invest matters more than how much you invest.

Two people. Alex starts investing $200 per month at age 22 and never stops. Jordan waits until 32, then invests $400 per month — twice as much — trying to catch up.

By age 65, at 7% annual return:
- Alex has approximately $914,000
- Jordan has approximately $791,000

Jordan invested more than twice as much money in dollar terms but still ends up with less. The early years, when Alex's contributions had the most time to compound, cannot be bought back.

This is not an argument for recklessness — it is an argument for starting something, anything, now. Even $50 per month. Even $20. The habit and the time horizon matter more than the amount.

## Where compound interest works against you

The same mathematics applies to debt. A credit card at 20% annual interest compounds against you just as aggressively as an investment compounds for you.

$5,000 of credit card debt left unpaid:
- After 5 years: $12,430
- After 10 years: $30,959

This is why paying off high-interest debt is almost always the right financial move before investing. The guaranteed 20% return from eliminating credit card debt beats any expected investment return.

Student loans in NZ are interest-free while you are earning below the repayment threshold — so these sit differently from credit card debt.

## Inflation: the silent tax

There is a counterforce to compound growth: inflation. If your money is sitting in a savings account earning 2% while inflation runs at 3%, you are losing purchasing power — your money buys less than it did a year ago, despite growing in dollar terms.

This is why keeping long-term savings in a high-interest savings account is not the same as investing. Cash savings are appropriate for your emergency fund and short-term goals. For long-term wealth building, you need returns that outpace inflation — which means investing in something with genuine growth potential, like a diversified share fund.

## The compounding of habits

Compound growth does not only apply to money. The habits you build in your twenties — around health, relationships, skills, and finances — also compound.

A person who reads consistently for 20 minutes a day accumulates vastly more knowledge over a decade than one who reads intensively for a month and then stops. Small, consistent actions over long periods produce outsized results.

Applied to money: $50 invested per week, every week, for 30 years is a different outcome from $50,000 invested once at age 50. The regularity and the time horizon are more powerful than the individual amounts.

## What this means practically

Start now. Whatever you can. Set up an automatic investment — even a small one — so that the decision does not rely on monthly willpower. And then leave it alone and let time work.

The best time to have started was ten years ago. The second best time is today.
