NZ Superannuation is one of the more straightforward parts of the NZ retirement system on paper — universal, not means-tested, paid from age 65 — but the details around residency and overseas pensions catch out more people than you would expect, often at exactly the point they are relying on the payment.

## Basic eligibility

To qualify for NZ Super you generally need to:

- Be 65 or older
- Be a NZ citizen, permanent resident, or hold a residence class visa
- Meet residency requirements — currently, having lived in NZ for at least 10 years since age 20, with at least 5 of those years since age 50 (rules have changed over time, so it is worth checking current requirements directly with Work and Income rather than relying on older information)

The residency requirement is where people who have spent significant time working or living overseas most often run into trouble — time spent overseas, even for NZ citizens, does not count toward the NZ residency requirement.

## Overseas pensions and direct deduction

If you are entitled to a pension from another country because you lived or worked there, NZ Super can be reduced dollar-for-dollar by the amount of that overseas pension, under what is called the "direct deduction" policy. This applies even if you have not yet applied for the overseas pension — in some cases you may be required to apply for an overseas entitlement you are eligible for before NZ Super is paid in full.

This is a frequently misunderstood area and one where the numbers can be significant. If you have lived or worked in the UK, Australia, or several other countries with reciprocal or comparable arrangements, it is worth getting a specific assessment from Work and Income well before you turn 65 rather than assuming both payments will simply add together.

## You have to apply — it is not automatic

Unlike some other government payments, NZ Super requires an application. Work and Income recommends applying around 12 weeks before your 65th birthday to avoid a gap in payments. You will need identification, proof of residency history, and details of any overseas pension entitlements. Applying online through Work and Income's website is generally the most straightforward route.

## Working while receiving NZ Super

A common misconception is that continuing to work past 65 reduces or removes your NZ Super. It does not — NZ Super is not means-tested against income or assets, so you can continue working full-time, part-time, or not at all, and receive the full payment regardless (subject to the tax code adjustments that apply once you are receiving both wages and NZ Super, since your overall tax rate may need adjusting). This makes NZ Super meaningfully different from some overseas systems, and is worth understanding clearly if you are planning a phased or gradual retirement rather than stopping work abruptly.

## Tax on NZ Super

NZ Super is taxable income, and if you continue to earn other income alongside it, you may need to use a specific tax code (commonly M SL or similar combinations depending on your situation) to make sure the right amount of tax is deducted across both income sources. Getting this wrong is common and usually results in a square-up at the end of the tax year rather than an ongoing problem, but it is worth flagging to IRD or a tax agent when your circumstances change.

## Practical timeline

- 12 to 18 months before turning 65: check your residency history and any overseas pension entitlements
- 3 months before turning 65: submit your NZ Super application
- Around your 65th birthday: confirm your payment has started and check the tax code being applied if you are also earning other income

Because the residency and overseas pension rules have genuine complexity for anyone who has spent time living or working abroad, this is one area of retirement planning where a direct conversation with Work and Income — not just general reading — is worth having well in advance.
