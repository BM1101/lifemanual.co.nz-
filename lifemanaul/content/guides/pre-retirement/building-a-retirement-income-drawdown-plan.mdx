Saving for retirement and living off those savings are genuinely different skills, and the second one gets far less attention despite being, in some ways, more complicated. A drawdown plan answers a different question than a savings plan — not "how much can I accumulate," but "how do I sequence withdrawals from multiple sources to last as long as I need them to."

## Start with your actual expected spending, not a rule of thumb

General rules like "you'll need 70% of your pre-retirement income" are a reasonable starting point but not a substitute for looking at your actual likely spending. Retirement spending is rarely flat across the whole period — it commonly follows a pattern:

- **Active years (roughly the first 10 to 15 years)** — often higher discretionary spending on travel, hobbies, and family, sometimes offset by lower costs like no longer commuting or supporting dependent children
- **Steady years** — spending typically settles into a stable, lower pattern as activity naturally reduces
- **Later years** — health and care-related costs often rise, sometimes significantly, particularly if residential care becomes necessary

Budgeting in these rough phases, rather than assuming a single flat annual figure for 25-plus years, produces a far more realistic and actionable plan.

## Sequencing your income sources

Most people in retirement are drawing from a combination of NZ Super, KiwiSaver, other investments, and sometimes rental income or part-time work. A sequencing plan considers:

- NZ Super as a stable base, arriving regardless of market conditions
- KiwiSaver and other investments as the flexible layer, ideally drawn down in a way that avoids being forced to sell growth assets during a market downturn
- Cash buffers (commonly 1 to 3 years of expenses held in low-risk, accessible accounts) specifically to avoid needing to sell investments at a bad time if markets are down when you need income

This last point — avoiding forced sales during downturns — is one of the more important and least understood parts of retirement drawdown, often called sequence of returns risk (covered in more detail in our related article on investment risk approaching retirement).

## The two most common mistakes

**Withdrawing too conservatively.** Many retirees, worried about running out of money, under-spend relative to what their savings could actually sustainably support — sometimes significantly. This is understandable but means working, saving, and sacrificing for years to fund a retirement that is then lived more frugally than necessary.

**Withdrawing too aggressively early.** The opposite mistake — spending heavily in the early, active years without a plan for how that affects the middle and later years — can create real shortfalls later, particularly if markets underperform in the meantime or health costs arrive earlier than expected.

A written plan, even a fairly simple spreadsheet modelling expected income sources against expected spending phases, helps avoid both mistakes by replacing vague anxiety or vague optimism with actual numbers.

## Building in flexibility

A good drawdown plan is not a fixed, unchangeable schedule — it should be revisited every year or two, adjusted for actual market performance, actual spending, and changing health or family circumstances. Some advisers recommend a "guardrails" approach, where you have a planned withdrawal rate but agree in advance to adjust it (spend a bit less, or allow a bit more) based on how your portfolio is actually tracking, rather than sticking rigidly to a single number regardless of circumstances.

## Getting help with the plan

Because a drawdown plan pulls together tax, investment, and NZ Super timing decisions, it is one of the areas where a session with a licensed financial adviser tends to be worth the cost — not because the individual pieces are necessarily complicated, but because getting the combination and sequencing right, and revisiting it as circumstances change, is easy to get wrong when managing it entirely alone.
