The decision between moving into a retirement village and staying in your own home as you age is as much a financial and legal decision as a lifestyle one, and the two options work in genuinely different ways — worth understanding clearly well before you need to decide under pressure.

## How retirement village agreements actually work

Unlike buying a standard property, most NZ retirement villages operate under an Occupation Right Agreement (ORA) rather than a freehold purchase. You pay a capital sum for the right to occupy a unit, but you generally do not own the property in the way a normal homeowner does, and you do not typically benefit from capital gains on the unit itself in the way you would with an owned home.

Key financial features to understand before signing:

- **Deferred management fee (DMF)** — a fee, often accruing at a percentage per year up to a cap (commonly around 20 to 30% total), that is deducted from your original payment when you eventually leave, whether by choice, moving to higher care, or death. This is one of the most misunderstood costs, since it is invisible day-to-day but significant at exit.
- **Weekly or fortnightly fees** — ongoing charges for village services, separate from the DMF, which continue regardless of the DMF
- **Refund timing** — many contracts do not refund your original capital until the unit is resold, which can take months and, in some contracts, longer — worth checking the specific terms rather than assuming a fixed timeframe

## The genuine appeal of retirement villages

Despite the financial complexity, villages offer real advantages for many people: a built-in community, on-site or nearby care options as needs increase, reduced home maintenance burden, and often a sense of security that comes from age-appropriate design and nearby support. For people whose own home has become difficult to maintain, or who are at risk of isolation, these are not minor benefits.

## The less obvious costs of staying in your own home

Staying in your own home is often assumed to be the cheaper option by default, but this deserves honest costing rather than assumption:

- Ongoing maintenance costs tend to rise as a home ages, and can become harder to manage or afford as mobility or income changes
- Modifications for accessibility (ramps, grab rails, bathroom changes) may be needed eventually and are worth budgeting for rather than treating as a surprise
- Isolation risk is real, particularly for those living alone or in areas with less nearby family or community support
- Home and contents insurance, rates, and utilities continue regardless of whether the home still genuinely suits your needs

None of this means staying home is the wrong choice — for many people it remains genuinely preferable — but it should be an informed comparison rather than an assumption.

## Get independent legal advice before signing anything

Because retirement village contracts differ so significantly from standard property purchases, and because the financial terms (DMF structure, refund timing, what happens if you need to move to higher care within the same village) vary considerably between providers, independent legal advice from a lawyer experienced in retirement village law is genuinely essential — not a box-ticking formality. Under the Retirement Villages Act, you are entitled to a disclosure statement and a mandatory cooling-off period, and using that time to get proper advice, rather than signing quickly under sales pressure, is worth the modest legal cost involved.

## A framework for deciding

Rather than treating this as village versus home in the abstract, it helps to separate the actual drivers: Is the concern primarily about future care needs, current home maintenance burden, social connection, or financial structure? Different drivers point toward different solutions — sometimes a smaller, more manageable standalone home solves the real problem better than either a village or staying in an unsuitable existing home, and this is worth including in the comparison rather than treating it as a binary choice.
