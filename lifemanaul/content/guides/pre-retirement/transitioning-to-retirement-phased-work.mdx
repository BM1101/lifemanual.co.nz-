The image of retirement as a single hard stop — full-time work one day, nothing the next — is common, but it is far from the only option, and for many people a phased transition produces a better financial and psychological outcome than an abrupt exit.

## Why a phased approach is worth considering

A gradual reduction in hours or responsibility, rather than a fixed retirement date, offers several practical advantages: continued income (even reduced) extends how long your savings need to last unassisted, continued KiwiSaver contributions (if still employed) keep building your balance for longer, and the structure and social connection of some ongoing work can ease the adjustment that a sudden full stop often makes harder than expected.

Since NZ Super is not means-tested and KiwiSaver access at 65 does not require you to stop working, there is no structural financial barrier to continuing to work in some capacity — the barriers are usually about what your current employer will agree to, or your own assumptions about what retirement is supposed to look like.

## Options worth raising with your employer

- **Reduced hours in your current role** — moving from full-time to three or four days a week, sometimes with a corresponding adjustment in scope, is increasingly accepted by NZ employers, particularly for experienced staff whose knowledge is valuable to retain
- **A lower-intensity role within the same organisation** — stepping back from a management or high-pressure position into a more advisory or specialist role can extend your working life in a way that suits both parties
- **Consulting or contracting** — moving from an employee arrangement to project-based or contract work gives more control over how much and when you work, though it comes with different tax (provisional tax) and lack of standard employment protections to be aware of
- **Mentoring or training arrangements** — some organisations formalise a transition period where experienced staff spend part of their time handing over knowledge to successors, which can be a genuinely valuable and lower-pressure final working phase

## How to raise this with an employer

Many employees assume a phased retirement request will be poorly received, but in practice, employers frequently prefer a planned, gradual transition over an abrupt departure that leaves a sudden knowledge or capacity gap. Framing the conversation around continuity and knowledge transfer, rather than purely around personal preference, tends to get a more constructive response. It is worth raising this well in advance — a year or more — rather than as a short-notice request, to give an employer genuine room to plan around it.

## The financial modelling side

A phased retirement changes your income and savings drawdown modelling considerably compared to a hard stop — reduced but continuing income means you may need to draw down savings more slowly, or not at all, during the transition years. This is worth incorporating into your broader retirement income drawdown plan (see our related article) rather than modelling as if full retirement begins on a single fixed date.

## The psychological side is real too

Retirement is a significant identity transition for many people, not just a financial one — the loss of routine, workplace social connection, and a sense of professional purpose is consistently identified in research as one of the more underestimated aspects of the change. A phased transition gives more time to build replacement routines, social connections, and sources of purpose gradually, rather than needing to construct an entirely new daily structure from a standing start on a single retirement date. This connects directly to the broader question of finding purpose after full-time work ends, covered in our related article.

## A reasonable default approach

Rather than assuming a single fixed retirement date is the only option, it is worth actively raising a phased approach with your employer at least a year or two before your planned retirement age, modelling the financial implications of reduced-but-continuing income, and treating the transition itself — not just the eventual full stop — as a deliberate, planned phase of your working life.
