The investment strategy that served you well through your accumulation years — decades of contributing to KiwiSaver or other investments while riding out market ups and downs — needs a genuine rethink as you approach the point where you start drawing that money down instead of adding to it.

## What sequence of returns risk actually means

The core idea is this: a market downturn early in your retirement (or in the final years before it) can do disproportionate damage compared to the same downturn happening earlier in your working life, because you are now withdrawing money from a shrinking balance rather than continuing to add to a growing one. Selling growth assets at a loss to fund living expenses locks in that loss in a way that simply waiting out the downturn while still working and contributing does not.

Two retirees with identical average investment returns over 20 years can end up in very different financial positions purely based on the order those returns occurred — a bad run in the first few years of retirement is considerably more damaging than the same bad run occurring later, even with identical long-term averages. This is why "average return" figures, while useful for long-term planning, do not tell the whole story for someone about to start drawing down their savings.

## Why shifting to fully conservative too early also has a cost

The natural response to sequence of returns risk is to shift entirely to cash or conservative assets well before retirement. This avoids the downturn risk but introduces a different, quieter cost: lost growth over what may be a 20 to 30 year retirement period, and reduced ability for your balance to keep pace with inflation over that time. Moving too conservative too early is a real and common mistake, not just an overly cautious "safe" choice.

## A more balanced approach

Rather than a single switch from growth to conservative on a fixed date, many advisers recommend a gradual glide path — progressively shifting the balance of your portfolio over the 5 to 10 years leading into retirement, and continuing to hold a meaningful growth component even after retirement begins, given how long the money may need to last. The specific balance depends on your total financial position, including how much of your retirement income comes from NZ Super (a stable, non-market-linked source) versus how much depends on investment drawdown.

## The cash buffer strategy

One of the more practical and widely used tools for managing this risk is holding a cash buffer — commonly one to three years of expected living expenses — in low-risk, easily accessible accounts, separate from your growth investments. The purpose is specific: if markets fall right when you need income, you draw from the cash buffer instead of selling growth assets at a loss, giving those assets time to recover before you need to touch them again. This does not eliminate market risk, but it meaningfully reduces the damage from bad timing.

## Rebalancing as you go

A sensible approach involves periodically rebalancing — topping up the cash buffer from growth assets during good market years, rather than only reacting during downturns. This requires a bit more active management than a fully passive "set and forget" approach, but is generally considered worth the effort given what is at stake in the drawdown phase specifically.

## Why this is worth professional advice

The right balance between growth and conservative assets, and the right size of cash buffer, depends heavily on your total financial position — how much of your income NZ Super covers, how much flexibility you have to adjust spending if markets underperform, and your own comfort with risk. This is a genuinely good area to get advice from a licensed financial adviser rather than applying a generic rule, since getting this specific balance wrong in either direction has real, lasting consequences for how long your money lasts.
