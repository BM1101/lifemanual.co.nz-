If your pay packet felt a little lighter after 1 April 2026, you are not imagining things. The minimum KiwiSaver contribution rate increased, and it is affecting every young worker on the default rate. Here is what changed, what it means in real dollars, and what you can actually do about it.

## What changed on 1 April 2026

From 1 April 2026, the minimum employer and employee KiwiSaver contribution rates increased to 3.5% (from 3%). This change was announced by the Government as part of its 2025 Budget and is designed to help New Zealanders grow stronger savings over time.

If you were contributing at the default rate of 3%, this automatically rose to 3.5% for both your contribution and your employer's. If you were already contributing more than 3%, your chosen rate stayed the same.

A further rise of the default rate from 3.5% to 4% is scheduled for 1 April 2028, so this is the first of two step-ups.

## How it affects your take-home pay

The extra 0.5% comes out of your gross (before-tax) pay. That means your take-home pay drops slightly, but the money goes straight into your KiwiSaver account — and your employer puts in more too.

Here is a rough guide based on common salaries:

- **$40,000 salary** — about $3.85 less per week in your pocket (extra $200/year into KiwiSaver from you, plus $200 from your employer)
- **$55,000 salary** — about $5.30 less per week (extra $275/year from you, plus $275 from your employer)
- **$70,000 salary** — about $6.75 less per week (extra $350/year from you, plus $350 from your employer)

For a typical earner, that is about $7 more per week from you, and about the same extra from your employer — so around $700 more in total would go into your KiwiSaver each year. Actual amounts will vary depending on pay cycles and payroll calculations.

The key point: every dollar you put in is matched by your employer, so the real benefit to your savings is double what you see leave your pay.

## Can you opt out of the increase?

You cannot opt out entirely, but a temporary rate reduction is available for people who want to carry on contributing at 3% from 1 April 2026 — you can apply for a temporary rate reduction for between 3 and 12 months.

Here is what you need to know about the temporary rate reduction:

- This is not the same as a hardship application — you do not need to demonstrate financial hardship to make the request.
- Your contributions will be reset to the default rate after 12 months, but you can apply for the rate reduction as many times as you like.
- You can apply for a temporary rate reduction at any time. You do not need to wait for a current rate reduction to end and can take them back-to-back.
- You can revoke your temporary rate reduction early — just talk to your employer.

To apply, log in to your myIR account on the Inland Revenue website (ird.govt.nz). Once you apply, Inland Revenue will send you an acceptance letter with the start and end dates of your temporary rate reduction that you will have to show your employer.

## The catch with opting down

Before you rush to reduce your rate, there is an important downside. If you apply for a temporary rate reduction to continue contributing at 3%, your employer's contribution could also stay at 3%. It is up to your employer to decide if they match your 3% or still contribute 3.5%. If they choose to stay on 3% too, effectively you are saving 1% less into your KiwiSaver account.

That is 1% of your gross salary each year that you and your employer are not putting toward your future. Over a 30- or 40-year career, with compound returns, that adds up to tens of thousands of dollars.

## Should you stay at 3.5% or reduce to 3%?

For most young workers, staying at 3.5% is the better move. Here is why:

- **Time is on your side.** While a half-percentage-point rise may sound modest, the impact over time can be meaningful. Higher regular contributions mean more money invested earlier, giving compound returns more time to work.
- **You get employer matching.** Your employer is required to put in 3.5% as well, which is essentially free money going into your savings.
- **It helps with the government contribution.** For the year ending 30 June 2026, the government contribution is calculated at 25 cents for every dollar of your contribution, up to a maximum of $260.72. You need to contribute $1,042.86 every year to receive the maximum.
- **The rate is going up again anyway.** The minimum contribution rate is scheduled to increase again to 4% from 1 April 2028. Adjusting now makes the next step smaller.

However, reducing to 3% might make sense if you are genuinely struggling with essential costs like rent, food, or debt repayments. There is no shame in using the temporary reduction — that is exactly what it is there for.

## What to do right now

1. **Check your payslip.** Contribution rates should have been updated in payroll systems from 1 April, so check your payslip to make sure the correct rate has been applied. If something does not look right, let your payroll team know.
2. **Decide whether 3.5% works for your budget.** Look at your actual weekly spending and see if the small drop in take-home pay is manageable.
3. **If you need to reduce, apply through myIR.** Go to ird.govt.nz and log in. The option is on the homepage once you have logged in.
4. **Consider going higher.** The available contribution rate options are 3%, 4%, 6%, 8%, or 10% of your gross salary or wages. If you can afford more than 3.5%, even bumping up to 4% now will put you ahead when the 2028 increase arrives.
5. **Review your fund type.** Make sure your fund type matches your time horizon and risk appetite. If you are in your twenties and not planning to withdraw for decades, a growth or balanced fund may suit you better than a conservative one.

## The bottom line

The KiwiSaver increase to 3.5% is a small weekly change that can make a big difference to your future. Even small increases can make a big difference over time as future returns are calculated on a larger balance — not just what you originally put in. If you can absorb the change, let it ride. If you genuinely cannot, the temporary rate reduction is a sensible safety valve — just be aware your employer might reduce their contribution too.

Your future self will thank you for every extra dollar you put in now.
