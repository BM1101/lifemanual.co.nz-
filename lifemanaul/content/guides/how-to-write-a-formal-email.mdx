Email is one of the primary ways professional communication happens, and being able to write a clear, well-structured email is a skill that will serve you throughout your career. Most people do not write emails particularly well — which means doing it well gets noticed.

## The basic structure

Every formal email has the same structure:

**Subject line** — specific and descriptive. "Question about the job" is vague. "Application for Saturday shift — Bella Morrison" is clear and searchable.

**Greeting** — "Dear [Name]," for formal emails. "Hi [Name]," is acceptable for semi-formal contexts (a teacher you know well, a colleague). Never "Hey" or no greeting at all.

**Opening line** — brief context if needed. "I am writing to enquire about..." or "Following up on our conversation last Tuesday..."

**Body** — your actual message. Be clear and specific. One paragraph per idea.

**Request or next step** — what do you want them to do? "Could you please let me know if..." or "I would appreciate it if you could..."

**Closing** — "Kind regards," "Regards," or "Thank you," followed by your full name.

## A template

```
Subject: [Specific and descriptive]

Dear [Name],

[One sentence of context if needed.]

[Your main message — what you need or are communicating.]

[Your specific request or next step.]

[Thank them if appropriate.]

Kind regards,
[Your full name]
[Your phone number if relevant]
```

## An example

**Bad version:**

> Subject: hi
>
> Hey Mr Thompson
>
> I was wondering about my essay mark because I thought I did pretty well on it and I'm curious what I got wrong. Let me know
>
> Thanks
> Jake

**Good version:**

> Subject: Essay feedback — Jake Williams, English 101
>
> Dear Mr Thompson,
>
> I hope you are well. I am writing to ask whether I could get some feedback on my recent essay. I would like to understand where I could improve for future assessments.
>
> Would it be possible to arrange a brief meeting at a time that suits you, or alternatively to receive written feedback if that is easier?
>
> Thank you for your time.
>
> Kind regards,
> Jake Williams

The good version is more likely to get a helpful response because it is clear, specific, shows consideration for the reader's time, and does not require them to do extra work to understand what you want.

## Key rules

**Use full sentences** — no dot points, no abbreviations, no text speak. Spell out "you" not "u", "before" not "b4".

**Keep it short** — most formal emails should be under 150 words. If your email is longer than that, ask yourself what you can cut. People are busy.

**One clear request** — if you are asking for something, make the request explicit. "I was wondering if maybe it might be possible to..." is vague. "Could you please..." is clear.

**Check your tone** — read it back before sending. Does it sound polite and professional? Would you be happy if a future employer saw it?

**Proofread** — spell check is not enough. Read the whole email before sending. Check names are spelled correctly. Check you have not written "pubic" instead of "public" (a genuinely common autocorrect error).

**Reply within 24 to 48 hours** — if someone emails you and you need time to respond properly, at least send a brief acknowledgment. "Thanks for your email — I'll get back to you by Thursday" is polite and buys you time.

## Subject lines

A good subject line:
- Describes exactly what the email is about
- Includes your name if you are a new contact
- Is specific enough that the recipient knows what to expect before opening it

Poor subject lines: "Hi", "Question", "Following up", "Important"

Good subject lines: "Work experience enquiry — Emma Tait, Rangitoto College", "Request to reschedule Thursday appointment — Jake Williams", "Invoice #1042 — due 30 May"

## When to use email vs other channels

Email is appropriate for: formal requests, applications, anything that needs a written record, communication with people you do not know.

Email is less appropriate for: urgent matters (call or text), casual chat with people you know well, things that need an immediate response.

If you email about something urgent, say so in the subject line: "Urgent: interview confirmation needed by 5pm today."

## Following up

If you have not received a reply after a week, it is appropriate to follow up with a brief, polite nudge:

> Subject: Following up — [original subject]
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> I wanted to follow up on my email from [date] regarding [topic]. Please let me know if you need any additional information from me.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Your name]

One follow-up is appropriate. Two is pushing it. Three is too many.
