A bank account is one of the first truly adult things you need. Without one, you can't get paid, you can't pay for things online, and you're essentially shut out of the financial system. The good news: opening one is straightforward, and in New Zealand most banks make it easy for young people.

## What age can you open an account?

In New Zealand, most banks will let you open a basic account from **age 13** with a parent or guardian's signature. From **age 16**, you can generally open an account in your own name without parental involvement, though the exact rules vary by bank.

If you're under 18, you'll usually be offered a "youth account" or "student account" — these typically have no monthly fees, which is exactly what you want.

## What you'll need to bring

Before you walk into a branch or start an online application, gather these:

- **Photo ID** — a passport is best. If you don't have one, a birth certificate plus another form of ID (school ID, community services card) usually works.
- **Proof of address** — a letter addressed to you (bank statement, utility bill, or letter from school). If you live with parents, a letter from them confirming your address is usually accepted.
- **IRD number** — your tax number. If you don't have one yet, you can still open the account — you just need to apply for an IRD number separately (see our guide: How to get your IRD number).
- **Parent or guardian** — if you're under 16, bring a parent or guardian who can sign the form.

<Callout>
You don't need money to open an account. You open it first, then deposit money into it.
</Callout>

## Choosing the right bank

The five main retail banks in New Zealand are ANZ, ASB, BNZ, Kiwibank, and Westpac. For a first account, the differences between them are small. What matters more is:

**Does it have a branch near you?** Useful when you need in-person help.

**Does it have a good app?** You'll manage most of your banking on your phone. Download a couple of the apps and see which you find easiest to use.

**Are there fees?** For under-18s, most banks offer completely fee-free accounts. Confirm this before you sign up — don't just assume.

**Is it Kiwibank?** Kiwibank is New Zealand-owned (owned by the government), which some people prefer. It's a solid option and has competitive youth accounts.

## Transaction account vs savings account

You actually want two accounts:

**Transaction account** (also called a cheque account or everyday account): This is where your money lives day-to-day. Your pay goes in, your spending goes out. It comes with an EFTPOS/debit card.

**Savings account**: A separate account where you put money you're saving. Typically earns a small amount of interest. You don't get a card for it — that's intentional. The friction of having to transfer money out means you're less likely to dip into it.

Most banks let you open both at the same time. Do it.

## What to watch out for

**Monthly account fees** — some accounts charge $5–$15 per month. At your stage, you should pay zero. If a bank tries to put you on a fee account, ask about their youth or student option.

**Overdraft** — some accounts automatically approve small overdrafts (going below zero). This can seem convenient but is essentially a mini-loan at high interest rates. Ask to have overdraft disabled if you're not confident about managing it.

**Interest on savings** — the interest rate on most savings accounts is very low (often 0.1–1%). This is fine for now — the point at this stage is building the habit of saving, not maximising returns. That comes later.

## Opening online vs in-branch

Most NZ banks now let you open an account entirely online or in their app. This is faster and works well if you have a NZ passport (the app can scan it). If you're using a birth certificate or have a more complicated situation, going into a branch is easier.

## After you open it

Once your account is open:

1. **Set up internet banking** — download the app and set up your login.
2. **Get your account number** — you'll need this to give to an employer when you get a job.
3. **Set up automatic transfers** — even $10/week automatically moving to your savings account builds a habit that pays off massively over time.
4. **Activate your card** — usually done in the app or at an ATM.

That's it. You're in the financial system. Next step: learning how to actually manage what goes through it — see our guide on Budgeting your first income.
