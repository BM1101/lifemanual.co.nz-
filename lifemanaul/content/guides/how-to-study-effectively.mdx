Most students study by re-reading their notes and hoping it sticks. Research on learning consistently shows this is one of the least effective methods available. This guide explains what actually works — and why.

## Why re-reading does not work

Re-reading creates a feeling of familiarity — the material looks familiar, so your brain tells you that you know it. But familiarity is not the same as being able to recall and use information under pressure.

The problem is that re-reading is passive. Your brain is not working hard to retrieve the information — it is just recognising it. And recognition is much easier than recall.

## What actually works: active recall

Active recall means testing yourself — trying to retrieve information from memory rather than recognising it when you see it. This forces your brain to work harder, which creates stronger, more durable memories.

**How to do it:**

- Cover your notes and try to write down everything you remember about a topic
- Use flashcards — write the question on one side, the answer on the other, and test yourself
- Do past exam papers without looking at your notes first
- Explain the topic out loud as if you are teaching someone who knows nothing about it

The moment of struggling to retrieve information is when the learning is happening. It feels harder than re-reading — which is exactly why it works.

## Spaced repetition

Spaced repetition means reviewing material over increasing time intervals rather than going over it once and moving on (or cramming everything the night before).

Research shows that the optimal time to review something is just before you are about to forget it. Reviewing it too soon wastes time. Waiting too long means you have already forgotten.

A simple version: review material the day after first learning it, then three days later, then a week later, then two weeks later. Each time you successfully recall it, the interval before the next review gets longer.

Apps like Anki automate this process — you rate how well you remembered something and it schedules the next review automatically.

## Other effective techniques

**Interleaving** — instead of studying one topic for two hours (blocked practice), mix topics within a study session. It feels harder and less comfortable, but research shows it leads to better long-term retention.

**Elaborative interrogation** — when you read something, ask yourself "why is this true?" and "how does this connect to what I already know?" Making connections between new and existing knowledge is one of the most powerful ways to learn.

**The Feynman Technique** — explain the concept in the simplest possible language, as if to a child. When you get stuck, go back to your notes, learn what you are missing, and try again. The gaps in your explanation show you exactly where the gaps in your understanding are.

## Environment matters

**Eliminate distractions** — put your phone in a different room or use an app that blocks distracting sites. Research shows that just having your phone visible (even face-down and silent) reduces cognitive capacity. It does not have to be in your hand to be a distraction.

**Study in the same place** — your brain associates environments with mental states. A consistent study space helps your brain shift into focus mode more quickly.

**Background noise** — some people work better in silence, some with background music. If you listen to music, instrumental works better than music with lyrics for most cognitive tasks. Coffee shop-style ambient noise (available on apps like Brain.fm) works well for some people.

**Study in chunks, not marathons** — the Pomodoro Technique is a popular method: 25 minutes of focused work, 5-minute break, repeat. After four cycles, take a longer break. This prevents mental fatigue and maintains focus.

## Before an exam

**Do not cram the night before** — last-minute cramming adds very little and costs you sleep. Sleep is when your brain consolidates memories. Going into an exam well-rested is more valuable than an extra hour of studying at midnight.

**Do past papers under exam conditions** — timed, no notes, in one sitting. This is the most accurate simulation of the actual exam and is more useful than almost any other form of preparation.

**On the day** — eat beforehand, arrive early, and if you feel anxious, take a few slow, deep breaths. Review the whole paper before starting and answer the questions you know first.

## A realistic approach

You do not have to implement all of this at once. Start with one thing: replace some of your re-reading time with self-testing. Fold a piece of paper to cover your notes and try to recall the key points. That single change, consistently applied, will make a meaningful difference.
