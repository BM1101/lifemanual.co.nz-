KiwiSaver is one of the best financial decisions available to young New Zealanders — and most teenagers do not know it exists until their first employer signs them up years later. The earlier you start, the dramatically better off you will be.

## What is KiwiSaver?

KiwiSaver is a voluntary savings scheme set up by the NZ government to help people save for retirement. When you contribute, your money is invested in a fund that grows over time. You cannot access the money until you turn 65 (with limited exceptions), which is the point — it is retirement savings.

Here is what makes it particularly good:

**Employer contributions** — if you are employed, your employer is legally required to contribute at least 3% of your gross wages on top of your pay. This is free money you only get if you are enrolled.

**Government contributions** — the government also contributes up to $521.43 per year, as long as you contribute at least $1,042.86 yourself in the year. This is also free money.

**Investment growth** — your contributions are invested and grow over time. The earlier you start, the more time compound growth has to work.

## Why starting young matters so much

The difference between starting KiwiSaver at 16 versus 25 is not just 9 extra years of contributions. It is the compound growth on those early contributions.

Money invested at 16 has roughly 49 years to grow before you reach 65. Money invested at 25 has 40 years. Those early years are the most valuable because the growth compounds — your returns earn returns, which earn returns.

Someone contributing $20 a week to KiwiSaver from age 16 will typically retire with significantly more than someone contributing $50 a week from age 25 — even though the second person contributed far more in dollar terms.

## Can you join before you start working?

Yes. You can join KiwiSaver from birth, but it works differently before you are employed:

**Under 18 and not working** — you can make voluntary contributions directly, but you do not get employer contributions (since you are not employed). The government member tax credit also has a minimum contribution threshold. Even small regular contributions make a difference over time.

**Under 18 and working** — if you are employed and earn more than $34,762 per year, you are automatically enrolled in KiwiSaver. If you earn less than this threshold, you can still opt in voluntarily, but you will not automatically be enrolled.

In practice, most teens should opt in voluntarily when they start their first job, even if the pay is low.

## How to join

The easiest way: ask your employer when you start work. They can enrol you and will handle the paperwork.

You can also enrol directly through a KiwiSaver provider (like ANZ, ASB, Fisher Funds, Simplicity, or many others) by going to their website and signing up. You will need your IRD number.

If you enrol through an employer, you will be placed in a default fund. You should actively choose your own fund rather than staying in the default.

## Choosing a fund

Your contributions go into a fund that invests in things like shares, bonds, and property. There are different fund types:

**Defensive or conservative** — mostly bonds and cash. Low risk, low growth. Appropriate for people close to retirement.

**Balanced** — mix of growth and defensive assets.

**Growth** — mostly shares. Higher risk short-term, but higher growth over long periods.

**Aggressive** — almost all shares. The highest risk and highest expected long-term growth.

If you are 16 to 40, you should almost certainly be in a growth or aggressive fund. You have decades before retirement, which means short-term market downturns do not matter — you have time to recover, and long-term the higher-growth funds significantly outperform conservative ones.

Sorted.org.nz has a KiwiSaver fund finder that helps you compare fees and returns across providers.

## Contribution rates

You can contribute 3%, 4%, 6%, 8%, or 10% of your gross pay. The minimum is 3%.

At 3%, your employer also contributes 3% (for a total of 6% of your salary going into KiwiSaver every pay cycle). Increasing your own contribution above 3% does not increase the employer contribution.

For most teenagers in part-time or low-income work, 3% is a sensible starting point. You can increase it later as your income grows.

## Making voluntary contributions

Even if you are not employed, you can make voluntary lump sum contributions directly to your KiwiSaver account. This is worth doing to hit the $1,042.86 threshold for the government member tax credit — effectively getting $521 for free every year.

If you contribute less than $1,042.86 in the year (July to June), you get a proportionally smaller government contribution. Contributing even $500 gets you roughly $260 in government contributions — still worth doing.

## The bottom line

Join KiwiSaver. Do it now, or when you start your first job. Choose a growth fund. Contribute at least enough to trigger the government contribution. Then forget about it for 40 years and let compound growth do its work.

There is almost no other financial decision with as favourable a risk-to-reward ratio available to a teenager in New Zealand.
