Most people think investing is for people with spare money, a financial advisor, and a confident understanding of the share market. None of those things are actually required. You can start today, with whatever you have, and the most important decision you'll make is simply to begin.

This guide covers everything a first-time investor in New Zealand needs to know — without jargon, without assuming prior knowledge, and without trying to sell you anything.

## Why invest at all?

Money sitting in a savings account loses value over time. Inflation — the general rise in prices — runs at roughly 2–3% per year in NZ. If your savings account pays 1%, you're effectively going backwards. Your $10,000 today will buy less in ten years than it buys now.

Investing puts your money to work at a rate that outpaces inflation. The NZ and global share markets have returned an average of roughly 7–10% per year over long periods (before inflation). That's not guaranteed — markets go up and down — but over decades, the direction has always been up.

The other reason to invest is compound growth. When your investments earn returns, those returns earn returns too. Small amounts invested early become large amounts over time — not because you invested a lot, but because you gave the money time to grow. See our guide on compound interest if you want to understand exactly how powerful this is.

## Step one: sort KiwiSaver first

Before you open any investment account, make sure you're getting the most out of KiwiSaver. Here's why: if you're employed, your employer is legally required to contribute at least 3% of your gross salary into your KiwiSaver on top of your wages. That's free money — and it's only available if you're contributing yourself.

The minimum employee contribution is 3%. If you're not contributing at least 3%, increase it now before doing anything else. You're leaving employer contributions on the table.

The government also adds up to $521.43 per year (the member tax credit) as long as you contribute at least $1,042.86 in the year. If you're not hitting that threshold, top it up before June 30 each year.

KiwiSaver is invested in a fund managed by your provider. If you've never actively chosen a fund, you're likely in a default conservative fund — which is almost certainly wrong for someone in their 20s or 30s. Log in to your KiwiSaver provider and check. If you're more than 10 years from retirement, you should be in a growth or aggressive fund. The difference in returns over 30 years is enormous.

## What is an index fund?

An index fund is an investment that tracks a market index — like the NZX 50 (the 50 largest companies listed on the NZ stock exchange) or the S&P 500 (the 500 largest US companies). When you invest in an index fund, you own a tiny slice of every company in that index.

This matters for two reasons:

**Diversification.** If one company collapses, it barely affects your investment because you own hundreds of others. You're not betting on any single business — you're betting on the entire economy, which over long periods has always grown.

**Low fees.** Index funds are passively managed — they just follow the index automatically, no expensive fund manager needed. Annual fees (called the management expense ratio, or MER) are typically 0.1–0.5%. Actively managed funds charge 1–2% and, on average, perform worse than index funds over the long run.

For a first-time investor, a simple global index fund is the right starting point for most people. It gives you exposure to thousands of companies across the world with a single investment.

## Where to actually invest in NZ

There are three main platforms for everyday New Zealanders:

**Sharesies** — the most beginner-friendly. You can start with $1, buy fractional shares and funds, and the app is clear and well-designed. Fees are a flat $3/month for portfolios under $50k, plus a small transaction fee. Good for getting started.

**InvestNow** — no platform fees at all. You invest directly with fund managers. A bit less polished than Sharesies but genuinely free to use, which matters a lot over decades. Minimum investment is $250 for most funds.

**Kernel** — NZ-built platform focused on low-cost index funds. Simple fund range, clear fee structure (~0.25% per year), no transaction fees. A strong option if you want to keep it simple.

All three are regulated by the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and hold client funds separately from company funds — meaning your money is protected if the platform goes under.

## What to actually buy

For most first-time investors, the answer is simple: **a global index fund.**

On Sharesies, look for Smartshares Total World or the Vanguard Total World ETF. On InvestNow or Kernel, look for a global equities index fund with an MER below 0.5%.

You don't need to split your money across multiple funds when you're starting out. One diversified global fund is enough. You can add complexity later — NZ shares, bonds, property — once you understand the basics and have more to invest.

## How much should you invest?

The honest answer: as much as you can afford, as regularly as possible.

A good starting framework is the 50/30/20 rule — 50% of take-home pay on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings and investment. If that feels impossible right now, start with whatever is possible — even $20/week is a start, and the habit matters more than the amount at this stage.

The most important thing is setting up an automatic transfer so the investment happens without you having to decide each time. Set it up once, then forget it. Monthly or fortnightly contributions into an index fund, automatically, is the entire strategy for most successful long-term investors.

## What about risk?

All investing involves risk. The value of your investments will go down sometimes — sometimes significantly. In 2020, global markets dropped 30% in a matter of weeks. In 2008, they fell 50%.

Both times, they recovered fully and went on to new highs. This is why time horizon matters. If you're investing money you might need in the next two years, it shouldn't be in the share market — keep it in a savings account or term deposit. If you're investing for ten years or more, short-term drops are noise.

The single worst thing you can do when markets fall is sell. That locks in your losses and means you miss the recovery. The correct action when markets fall is to do nothing — or ideally, to keep contributing, because you're now buying at lower prices.

## What investing is not

It's not trading. Buying and selling shares frequently based on news or hunches is gambling, not investing, and the evidence that anyone does it successfully over long periods is thin. The strategy described in this guide — regular contributions to a diversified index fund, held for decades — outperforms most active traders and almost all fund managers over time.

It's not get-rich-quick. Investing works slowly and then quickly, thanks to compound growth. The first ten years feel unremarkable. The last ten years feel extraordinary. You have to earn the last ten by surviving the first ten.

It's not something to delay until you have more money. The cost of waiting is real and significant. Use the calculator above to see exactly what delaying by five years costs you in final balance.

## Getting started today: the checklist

1. Log into KiwiSaver — check your fund type and contribution rate
2. If you're in a conservative or default fund and more than 10 years from retirement, switch to growth or aggressive
3. Make sure you're contributing enough to get your full employer match (minimum 3%)
4. Open an account on Sharesies, InvestNow, or Kernel — takes about 10 minutes
5. Set up an automatic contribution — even $50/fortnight
6. Choose a global index fund with a low MER
7. Set a calendar reminder to check in once a year — not more often

That's the whole strategy. It's not exciting, which is exactly why it works.
