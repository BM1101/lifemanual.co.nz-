Most people think investing is for people with spare money, a financial advisor, and a confident understanding of the share market. None of those things are actually required. You can start today, with whatever you have, and the most important decision you will make is simply to begin.

This guide covers everything a first-time investor in New Zealand needs to know — without jargon, without assuming prior knowledge, and without trying to sell you anything.

## Why invest at all?

Money sitting in a savings account loses value over time. Inflation — the general rise in prices — runs at roughly 2–3% per year in NZ. If your savings account pays 1%, you are effectively going backwards. Your $10,000 today will buy less in ten years than it buys now.

Investing puts your money to work at a rate that outpaces inflation. The NZ and global share markets have returned an average of roughly 7–10% per year over long periods. That is not guaranteed — markets go up and down — but over decades, the direction has always been up.

The other reason to invest is compound growth. When your investments earn returns, those returns earn returns too. Small amounts invested early become large amounts over time — not because you invested a lot, but because you gave the money time to grow.

## Step one: sort KiwiSaver first

Before you open any investment account, make sure you are getting the most out of KiwiSaver. If you are employed, your employer is legally required to contribute at least 3% of your gross salary on top of your wages. That is free money — and it is only available if you are contributing yourself.

The government also adds up to $521.43 per year as long as you contribute at least $1,042.86 in the year. If you are not hitting that threshold, top it up before June 30 each year.

If you have never actively chosen a KiwiSaver fund, you are likely in a default conservative fund. Log in and check. If you are more than 10 years from retirement, you should be in a growth or aggressive fund. The difference in returns over 30 years is enormous.

## What is an index fund?

An index fund is an investment that tracks a market index — like the NZX 50 or the S&P 500. When you invest in one, you own a tiny slice of every company in that index.

This matters for two reasons. First, diversification — if one company collapses, it barely affects your investment because you own hundreds of others. Second, low fees — index funds are passively managed, so annual fees are typically 0.1–0.5%, compared to 1–2% for actively managed funds that on average perform worse.

For a first-time investor, a simple global index fund is the right starting point for most people.

## Where to actually invest in NZ

**Sharesies** — the most beginner-friendly. Start with $1, buy fractional shares and funds, clean app. Fees are $3/month for portfolios under $50k plus a small transaction fee.

**InvestNow** — no platform fees at all. You invest directly with fund managers. A bit less polished but genuinely free to use, which matters a lot over decades. Minimum investment is $250 for most funds.

**Kernel** — NZ-built platform focused on low-cost index funds. Simple fund range, clear fee structure around 0.25% per year, no transaction fees.

## What to actually buy

For most first-time investors, the answer is simple: a global index fund.

On Sharesies, look for Smartshares Total World or the Vanguard Total World ETF. On InvestNow or Kernel, look for a global equities index fund with an annual fee below 0.5%.

You do not need to split your money across multiple funds when starting out. One diversified global fund is enough. You can add complexity later once you understand the basics.

## How much should you invest?

As much as you can afford, as regularly as possible. A good starting point is the 50/30/20 rule — 50% of take-home pay on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on saving and investing. If that feels impossible, start with whatever is possible. Even $20 per week is a start.

The most important thing is setting up an automatic transfer so the investment happens without you having to decide each time. Monthly or fortnightly contributions into an index fund, automatically — that is the entire strategy for most successful long-term investors.

## What about risk?

All investing involves risk. The value of your investments will go down sometimes — sometimes significantly. In 2020, global markets dropped 30% in weeks. In 2008, they fell 50%. Both times, they recovered fully and went on to new highs.

This is why time horizon matters. If you are investing money you might need in the next two years, keep it in a savings account. If you are investing for ten years or more, short-term drops are noise.

The single worst thing you can do when markets fall is sell. That locks in your losses and means you miss the recovery. The correct action when markets fall is to do nothing — or keep contributing, because you are now buying at lower prices.

## Getting started today

1. Log into KiwiSaver — check your fund type and contribution rate
2. If you are in a conservative fund and more than 10 years from retirement, switch to growth or aggressive
3. Make sure you are contributing enough to get your full employer match
4. Open an account on Sharesies, InvestNow, or Kernel — takes about 10 minutes
5. Set up an automatic contribution — even $50 per fortnight
6. Choose a global index fund with a low annual fee
7. Set a calendar reminder to check in once a year — not more often

That is the whole strategy. It is not exciting, which is exactly why it works.
