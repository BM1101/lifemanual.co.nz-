Most people think investing requires spare money, a financial advisor, and knowledge of the share market. None of those are actually required. This guide explains exactly what to do as a first-time investor in New Zealand — without jargon and without trying to sell you anything.

## Step one: sort KiwiSaver first

Before opening any investment account, make sure you are getting the most out of KiwiSaver. If you are employed, your employer contributes at least 3% of your gross wages on top of your pay. The government also contributes up to $521 per year if you contribute at least $1,042 yourself.

That combination — employer contributions plus government contributions — is the best return on investment available to most New Zealanders. No share market comes close.

If you are not already contributing enough to get your full employer match, start there.

Also check which KiwiSaver fund you are in. Most young people should be in a growth or aggressive fund — not a conservative one. Log into your KiwiSaver provider and check.

## What is an index fund?

An index fund is an investment that automatically tracks a market index — like the NZX 50 (the 50 largest NZ companies) or the S&P 500 (the 500 largest US companies). When you invest in an index fund, you own a tiny fraction of every company in that index.

Two reasons this is the right starting point for most people:

**Diversification** — you own hundreds of companies, so no single company failing wrecks your investment.

**Low fees** — index funds are passively managed (they just follow the index, no expensive analysts needed). Annual fees are typically 0.1 to 0.5%, versus 1 to 2% for actively managed funds — which, on average, perform worse anyway.

For a first investment, a simple global index fund gives you exposure to thousands of companies across the world in one purchase.

## Where to actually invest in NZ

**Sharesies (sharesies.nz)**
The most beginner-friendly option. You can start with $1. Clean app, easy to use, good range of funds and shares. Fees: $3 per month for portfolios under $50,000, plus a small transaction fee (0.5% up to $3,000, capped at $5 above that).

**InvestNow (investnow.co.nz)**
No platform fees — you invest directly with fund managers. Slightly less polished than Sharesies but genuinely free to use, which matters over decades. Minimum $250 per fund for most funds.

**Kernel (kernelwealth.com)**
NZ-built platform focused on low-cost index funds. Simple fund range, annual fee around 0.25%, no transaction fees. Clean and easy to stick with long-term.

**Hatch (hatchinvest.nz)**
Good for investing directly in US-listed shares and ETFs if you want exposure to international markets. USD-denominated.

None of these are sponsored. All are regulated by the Financial Markets Authority (FMA).

## What to actually buy

For most first-time investors: **a global index fund**.

On Sharesies: Smartshares Total World ETF or Vanguard Total World ETF (available via Hatch).
On InvestNow: Vanguard or a global equities fund with fees below 0.5%.
On Kernel: the Global 100 or similar.

You do not need multiple funds when starting out. One diversified global fund is enough. Complexity can come later — simplicity is more likely to be maintained.

## How much to invest

As much as you can, as regularly as possible. Even $20 per week invested consistently in an index fund over decades becomes significant. The amount matters less than the habit.

A useful framework: pay yourself first. When your pay comes in, immediately move your investment amount to your investment account before spending anything. Adjust your spending to what is left, rather than investing what is left (which is usually nothing).

## What you need to know about risk

Investing in shares means your balance will go up and down. In any given year the market might fall 10%, 20%, or more. In 2020 it fell 30% in a few weeks.

This is normal. Markets have always recovered from downturns and gone on to new highs. If you are investing for 10 or more years, short-term drops are noise — what matters is the long-term trend.

The worst thing you can do is sell when markets fall. That locks in your loss and means you miss the recovery. The correct response to a market downturn is to do nothing. Ideally, keep contributing — you are buying at lower prices.

## Tax on investments

Investment returns are taxable in NZ. Different structures are taxed differently:

**PIE funds (Portfolio Investment Entity)** — most managed funds and ETFs are PIEs. Tax is paid automatically by the fund at your Prescribed Investor Rate (PIR), which is based on your income. You do not need to do anything extra.

**Direct share ownership** — dividends are taxable income. Capital gains on shares are generally not taxed in NZ (unlike many countries), though this applies only to shares held as investments, not as a business.

When you open an account, you will be asked for your PIR. Use the correct rate — too low and you will owe tax later, too high and you overpay.

## Getting started checklist

1. Log into your KiwiSaver — check your fund type and contribution rate
2. Open an account on Sharesies, InvestNow, or Kernel (takes 10 minutes)
3. Set your PIR
4. Choose a global index fund with fees below 0.5%
5. Set up an automatic contribution — even $25 per week
6. Check in once a year, not more often

That is the entire strategy. It is not exciting, which is exactly why it works over long periods.
