The internet is one of the most useful tools in human history. It is also a place where decisions made in a few seconds can have consequences that last for years. This guide is about being smart online — not scared, but informed.

## What is a digital footprint?

Your digital footprint is the record of everything you do online. It includes:

- Posts, photos, videos, and comments you publish
- Things you are tagged in by others
- Accounts you create
- Things you search for
- Reviews and ratings you leave

Some of this is public and searchable. Some of it is technically private but can be screenshotted, shared, or accessed. The key principle is this: **assume that anything you post online could eventually be seen by anyone.**

## Why it matters more than you think

Most 12-year-olds are not thinking about job applications. But the digital footprint you build now will still exist when you are 22, 30, or 40.

Employers and universities routinely search candidates online. A photo, comment, or post that seemed funny at the time can create a genuinely bad first impression years later. This does not mean you cannot have fun or be yourself online — it means being thoughtful about what you put out there permanently.

It also matters right now. Screenshots of private messages get shared. Posts made in anger or as a joke can spread further than intended. Things shared within a friend group have a way of getting out.

## Practical habits that protect you

**Use strong, unique passwords** — a strong password is long (12+ characters), random, and not used anywhere else. Use a password manager (like Bitwarden, which is free) to remember them.

**Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA)** — this means that even if someone gets your password, they cannot get into your account without a second verification (usually a code sent to your phone). Turn it on for every account that matters — email, Instagram, Snapchat, gaming accounts.

**Check your privacy settings** — most social media platforms default to more public settings than most people realise. Go into your settings and check who can see your posts, who can tag you, and who can find you by phone number or email.

**Think before you post** — a useful test: would you be comfortable if your grandparent, future employer, and worst enemy all saw this? If the answer is no, reconsider.

**Do not share personal information publicly** — your school, suburb, phone number, or daily routine does not need to be public.

**Be careful with location sharing** — many apps share your location by default. Check which apps have access to your location and whether they need it.

## Cyberbullying and online harassment

If someone is saying something about you online that is hurtful, threatening, or untrue:

- Take screenshots as evidence
- Block and report the person on the platform
- Do not respond — engaging usually makes it worse
- Tell a trusted adult — a parent, teacher, or school counsellor

You do not have to deal with this alone, and in serious cases it can be reported to police. In New Zealand, the Harmful Digital Communications Act makes it illegal to send messages intended to cause harm.

You can also contact Netsafe (netsafe.org.nz or 0508 638 723) for free, confidential advice about online incidents.

## If someone asks for photos or personal information

Any adult who asks a teenager for photos of themselves or personal information online is behaving inappropriately, regardless of how friendly or normal they seem. This is true even if they claim to be a peer.

If this happens to you, do not respond, block the person, and tell a trusted adult immediately. You will not get in trouble for telling someone.

## The bigger picture

Most of your online interactions will be perfectly fine. The goal is not to be paranoid — it is to be aware. The internet is a genuinely great place to learn things, connect with people, express yourself, and build skills. Doing that with some basic awareness of how digital footprints work means you can enjoy it without creating problems for your future self.
