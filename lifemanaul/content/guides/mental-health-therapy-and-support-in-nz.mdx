About one in five New Zealanders experiences a mental health problem in any given year. That number is higher for young adults. Anxiety, depression, and stress-related conditions are extremely common — not a sign of weakness or personal failure, and not something you just have to endure.

This guide explains what support is available, how to access it, and what to expect.

## How to know if you need support

There is no precise threshold. If you are wondering whether what you are experiencing is serious enough to get help, that question itself is a reason to talk to someone.

Some signs it is time to seek support:

- Persistent low mood or loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, for more than two weeks
- Anxiety that is significantly affecting your daily life — work, relationships, or activities
- Difficulty sleeping or sleeping excessively
- Using alcohol or other substances more than you would like
- Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
- Feeling unable to cope with ordinary life demands
- Significant changes in appetite or weight
- Withdrawal from friends and activities

You do not need to be in crisis to benefit from support. Therapy and counselling are also useful for navigating difficult life transitions, relationship challenges, grief, and building general psychological resilience.

## Starting with your GP

Your GP is the best first point of contact for most mental health concerns. They can:

- Assess your symptoms and diagnose conditions like depression and anxiety
- Prescribe medication if appropriate
- Refer you to publicly funded mental health services
- Connect you with a counsellor or psychologist

Being honest with your GP is important. Describe what you are actually experiencing, not a minimised version of it. They have heard everything before — there is nothing that will shock them or cause you to be judged.

## Free and low-cost options

**1737 — Need to talk?**
Free, 24/7 counselling by phone or text. Text or call 1737 any time. Trained counsellors, completely confidential.

**Your GP**
As described above. The consultation cost applies (reduced if you have a Community Services Card), but GP referrals open the door to funded services.

**Primary Mental Health and Addiction services**
Many GP practices have access to funded counselling or psychology sessions for mild to moderate mental health conditions. Ask your GP whether this is available at your practice — sessions are often free or low-cost.

**Student health services**
If you are a tertiary student, your institution likely has a student health service offering counselling at low or no cost. These are generally good quality and under-utilised.

**EAP (Employee Assistance Programme)**
If you are employed, your employer may have an EAP which offers free confidential counselling sessions — typically 3 to 6 sessions per year. Ask your HR team whether this exists at your workplace.

**Youthline**
0800 376 633 or text 234. Free support for young people, not just crisis support — you can talk about anything.

**Lifeline**
0800 543 354. 24/7 crisis and emotional support.

## Publicly funded mental health services

For moderate to severe mental health conditions, the public mental health system provides funded specialist treatment — psychiatrists, psychologists, and community mental health teams. Access is typically through GP referral or via community mental health crisis teams.

Waiting lists can be long for non-urgent referrals. Being honest about the severity of what you are experiencing helps ensure you are appropriately prioritised.

## Private therapy

Private therapists (psychologists, counsellors, psychotherapists) see clients without a referral, at your own cost. Sessions typically cost $100 to $200 per hour.

If this is financially difficult, ask therapists whether they offer a sliding scale fee based on income — many do, particularly in the counselling space. Community counselling organisations (such as Relationships Aotearoa or local community counselling services) often offer subsidised rates.

## What to expect from therapy

Different types of therapy work differently. The most evidence-based for anxiety and depression is Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT), which focuses on identifying and changing unhelpful thought patterns and behaviours.

In general: therapy requires you to do work. It is not passive. Sessions involve talking about your experience, understanding patterns in your thinking and behaviour, and trying new approaches. Most people notice a difference within 6 to 12 sessions, though this varies.

The relationship with your therapist matters enormously. If after several sessions you do not feel like the therapist is the right fit, it is okay to seek someone different.

## If you or someone you know is in crisis

**If there is immediate risk of harm:** Call 111.

**Crisis support:**
- 1737: text or call
- Lifeline: 0800 543 354
- Suicide Crisis Helpline: 0508 828 865

If you are concerned about someone else, ask them directly whether they are thinking about suicide. This does not plant the idea — research shows that asking directly reduces risk by opening up the conversation.
